Hash-based SNARKs from zero

    Proximity gaps

    What it means to prove that a word is close to a Reed-Solomon code, why a random fold keeps far words far, and how FRI and WHIR turn that one fact into a proof system.

    You need: arithmetic modulo a prime, and polynomials (a polynomial of degree less than \(k\) is fixed by its values at \(k\) points).
    You will build: Reed-Solomon codes, distance and its three radii, list decoding, proximity gaps, correlated agreement, mutual correlated agreement, and the FRI and WHIR protocols that rely on them.
    Colours: green is the code and its codewords, red an error, blue and purple the words \(u\) and \(v\) a prover commits to, teal a folded word, yellow the verifier's randomness, orange a radius.

    1Spot checks see far, not wrong

    A SNARK is a short proof that a computation was done correctly, which a verifier checks far faster than redoing the computation. A hash-based SNARK ends the same way every time. The prover has written down a long list of field elements, a word, and committed to it with a Merkle root. The verifier wants to know that the word is a list of values of a low-degree polynomial. It can afford to read a few dozen entries, out of millions.

    Figure 1 shows what a few reads can and cannot see. The word holds the values of one polynomial at 16 points, and three reads are made.

    The verifier in Figure 1 was even given the right word to compare against, which a real verifier never has. Still it misses the single change most of the time. A few reads can only ever answer a weaker question: is the word far from every acceptable word? Making "far" and "acceptable" precise, and answering that question with a handful of reads, is the subject of this page.

    2Reed-Solomon codes

    The acceptable words are the evaluations of low-degree polynomials. Our running example works in the field \(\mathbb{F}_{17}\) (integers modulo 17) and evaluates at every non-zero element, the 16 points \(L = \{1, 2, \dots, 16\}\).

    Definition 1 (Reed-Solomon code)

    Let \(\mathbb{F}\) be a finite field, \(L \subseteq \mathbb{F}\) a set of \(n\) points (the domain) and \(k \le n\). The Reed-Solomon code is \[ \class{c-green}{\mathrm{RS}[\mathbb{F}, L, k]} = \bigl\{\, (p(x))_{x \in L} \;:\; p \in \mathbb{F}[X],\ \deg p < k \,\bigr\} \subseteq \mathbb{F}^{L}. \] Its elements are codewords; \(n\) is the length, \(k\) the dimension and \(\rho = k/n\) the rate.

    We take \(k = 4\): codewords are the value lists of cubics, and the rate is \(\rho = 4/16 = 1/4\). Our main codeword is \[ \class{c-green}{f(x)} = 3 + x + 4x^2 + x^3, \qquad \bigl(f(1), \dots, f(16)\bigr) = (9, 12, 1, 16, 12, 12, 5, 14, 11, 2, 10, 7, 16, 9, 9, 5). \] For instance \(f(2) = 3 + 2 + 16 + 8 = 29 = 12 \pmod{17}\). Figure 2 draws a word as 16 columns, one per point of \(L\), with its value as the height of a dot. A green ring marks where the codeword is.

    That redundancy is what lets a code detect changes. Two distinct codewords cannot agree in many places: their difference is a non-zero polynomial of degree less than \(k\), which has fewer than \(k\) roots. Figure 3 takes a second codeword \(\class{c-purple}{g} = f + 5(x-2)(x-7)(x-11)\) and counts.

    Fact 1 (minimum distance)

    Two distinct codewords of \(\mathrm{RS}[\mathbb{F}, L, k]\) agree on at most \(k - 1\) points, so they differ in at least \(d = n - k + 1\) points. In relative terms the code's distance is \(d/n \approx 1 - \rho\).

    The code is also linear: sums and multiples of codewords are codewords, because the same holds for polynomials of degree less than \(k\). In the running example \(d = 16 - 4 + 1 = 13\). A low rate buys a large distance: at \(\rho = 1/4\), codewords differ in more than three quarters of their entries.

    3Distance, closeness and three radii

    "Far" needs a ruler. The ruler is the count of disagreeing entries.

    Definition 2 (distance to a code)

    For words \(w, c \in \mathbb{F}^L\), the relative Hamming distance is \(\Delta(w, c) = \tfrac{1}{n}\,\bigl|\{x \in L : w(x) \ne c(x)\}\bigr|\). The distance from \(w\) to a code \(\mathcal{C}\) is \(\Delta(w, \mathcal{C}) = \min_{c \in \mathcal{C}} \Delta(w, c)\). The word \(w\) is \(\delta\)-close to \(\mathcal{C}\) if \(\Delta(w, \mathcal{C}) \le \delta\), and \(\delta\)-far otherwise. The list \(\mathrm{List}(w, \delta)\) is the set of codewords within \(\delta\) of \(w\).

    Equivalently, \(w\) is \(\delta\)-close when some codeword agrees with it on a set \(S \subseteq L\) of size \(|S| \ge (1 - \delta)n\): (the agreement set \(S\), with the errors outside it).

    How far may a word drift before its codeword becomes ambiguous? Three thresholds answer this, and each one will matter. The unique decoding radius \(\tfrac{1-\rho}{2}\) is half the distance: two codewords cannot both lie within it, because their mutual distance is at least \(1-\rho\). The Johnson radius \(1 - \sqrt{\rho}\) is the radius up to which the Johnson bound (Theorem 1) guarantees short lists, for any code with this distance. The capacity \(1 - \rho\) is the limit for every code of rate \(\rho\): beyond it some words have exponentially long lists. Figure 4 places them for every rate.

    For our code, \(\rho = 1/4\) gives \(\tfrac{1-\rho}{2} = 0.375\) (up to 6 errors out of 16), \(1 - \sqrt{\rho} = 0.5\) (fewer than 8) and \(1 - \rho = 0.75\) (fewer than 12). Figure 5 builds a word on the boundary between the first two regimes: it equals \(f\) on the points 1 to 8 and \(g\) on the points 9 to 16.

    Theorem 1 (Johnson bound)

    For every word \(w\) and every \(\eta \in (0, 1-\sqrt{\rho})\), \(\ \bigl|\mathrm{List}(w,\, 1 - \sqrt{\rho} - \eta)\bigr| \le \dfrac{1}{2\eta\sqrt{\rho}}\).

    At 7 errors, \(\delta = 7/16\) and \(\eta = 1/2 - 7/16 = 1/16\), so the bound allows \(1/(2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{16} \cdot \tfrac12) = 16\) codewords; Figure 5 finds 2. The bound is loose, but it is a bound, and protocols that work in the list regime use it to count what a cheating prover could hide behind.

    4Why a SNARK tests closeness

    In a hash-based SNARK the prover encodes its witness polynomial \(p\) as the codeword \((p(x))_{x \in L}\), commits to it with a Merkle tree, and later answers queries with authentication paths. Figure 6 runs that exchange.

    The obvious test would be: read some entries and check they lie on a polynomial of degree less than \(k\). Figure 7 shows why that test is useless below \(k + 1\) reads.

    So a Reed-Solomon proximity test needs help from the prover: extra committed words and a short interaction that shrink the problem. Such an interaction is an interactive oracle proof of proximity (IOPP).

    Definition 3 (proximity test)

    The verifier has oracle access to committed words: it reads them only at positions it queries. An IOPP for \(\mathcal{C}\) with proximity parameter \(\delta\) and soundness error \(\varepsilon\) is an interaction in which the verifier reads the committed word \(w\) only at a few positions, and:

    • completeness: if \(w \in \mathcal{C}\), the honest prover makes the verifier accept;
    • soundness: if \(\Delta(w, \mathcal{C}) > \delta\), every prover makes it accept with probability at most \(\varepsilon\).

    Words between "in the code" and "\(\delta\)-far" get no promise. That gap costs nothing, for two reasons. If \(\delta\) is below the unique decoding radius, a \(\delta\)-close word determines exactly one polynomial, and the rest of the SNARK argues about that polynomial. Above it, the word determines a short list (Theorem 1), and the protocol either proves its claims for every member of the list (list binding, as in leanVM) or adds a check that pins one member down (Section 8).

    Closeness tests also carry claims about the polynomial. To prove \(p(z) = y\), the prover shows that the quotient \(q(x) = \dfrac{p(x) - y}{x - z}\) is close to a code of dimension \(k - 1\). If \(y = p(z)\) the division is exact and \(q\) is a polynomial of degree \(k - 2\). Otherwise \(q\) is not a polynomial at all. Figure 8 tries every claim \(y\) for \(z = 5\), where \(f(5) = 12\).

    Why can the distance in Figure 8 never exceed 12?

    The code has dimension 3 on 15 points. Any 3 entries of the quotient are matched by some quadratic, so the nearest quadratic agrees on at least 3 points and differs on at most \(15 - 3 = 12\).

    5Folding, and the proximity gap

    Every proximity test shrinks its problem with the same move. Given two words \(\class{c-blue}{u}, \class{c-purple}{v}\), the verifier sends a random \(\class{c-yellow}{\alpha} \in \mathbb{F}\), and from then on only the single word \[ \class{c-teal}{u + \alpha v} \] is tested. A random linear combination of many words works the same way. Figure 9 introduces the compact drawing used from here on: a word as a strip of 16 cells, red where it disagrees with its nearest codeword.

    The fold is useful only if it is honest about its inputs: when \(u\) or \(v\) is far from the code, \(u + \alpha v\) should be far too. It cannot be far for every \(\alpha\) (at \(\alpha = 0\) it is \(u\) itself), so the right demand is for almost every \(\alpha\). Figure 10 shows all 17 folds of three pairs.

    Figure 10 shows the shape of the main theorem. For a pair \((u, v)\), the set of \(\alpha\) with \(u + \alpha v\) \(\delta\)-close is either everything or almost nothing. There is a gap between the two.

    Which pairs are close for every \(\alpha\)? Not merely pairs where \(u\) and \(v\) are each close: the separately close pair fails. They must be close on the same set of positions.

    Definition 4 (correlated agreement)

    Words \(u, v\) have \(\delta\)-correlated agreement with \(\mathcal{C}\) if there are codewords \(c_0, c_1\) and one set \(\class{c-green}{S} \subseteq L\) with \(|S| \ge (1-\delta)n\) such that \(u = c_0\) and \(v = c_1\) on \(S\).

    If \(u, v\) have \(\delta\)-correlated agreement, then every fold agrees with the codeword \(c_0 + \alpha c_1\) on \(S\), so every fold is \(\delta\)-close. The theorem is the converse.

    Theorem 2 (proximity gap and correlated agreement; BCIKS20, BCHKS25)

    Let \(\mathcal{C} = \mathrm{RS}[\mathbb{F}, L, k]\) and \(\delta < 1 - \sqrt{\rho}\). There is an \(\varepsilon\) such that for all \(u, v \in \mathbb{F}^L\): \[ \Pr_{\class{c-yellow}{\alpha} \leftarrow \mathbb{F}}\Bigl[\, \Delta(\class{c-teal}{u + \alpha v}, \mathcal{C}) \le \class{c-orange}{\delta} \,\Bigr] > \varepsilon \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u, v \text{ have } \delta\text{-correlated agreement.} \] In the unique decoding regime \(\varepsilon\) is about \(n/|\mathbb{F}|\) (BCIKS20). Up to the Johnson radius BCIKS20 prove \(O(n^2/|\mathbb{F}|)\), and BCHKS25 improve it to \(a \cdot n/|\mathbb{F}|\), with \(a\) growing as \(\delta\) approaches \(1 - \sqrt{\rho}\).

    Read contrapositively: if \(u\) or \(v\) is \(\delta\)-far (so they cannot have correlated agreement), the fold is \(\delta\)-far except with probability \(\varepsilon\). With \(n = 2^{22}\) and \(|\mathbb{F}| \approx 2^{128}\), \(\varepsilon\) is around \(2^{-100}\) or smaller. In \(\mathbb{F}_{17}\) it is useless (\(n/|\mathbb{F}| \approx 1\)). Drag Figure 10's radius to 8 on the far pair: two folds come within 8 errors. Radius 8 is \(\delta = 1/2\), the Johnson radius itself, where the theorem has stopped applying, so the toy fails both of its hypotheses there.

    Where does the shared set come from? Figure 11 runs the easy half of the argument, which uses only two close folds and loses a factor of 2.

    Example 1 (the algebra of Figure 11)

    1. On \(S\), \(w_1 = c'_1\) and \(w_2 = c'_2\) for codewords \(c'_1, c'_2\).
    2. Subtract: \(w_1 - w_2 = (4 - 2)v\), so on \(S\), \(v = \tfrac{1}{2}(c'_1 - c'_2)\), a codeword because the code is linear.
    3. Then \(u = w_1 - 4v = c'_1 - 2(c'_1 - c'_2)\) on \(S\), also a codeword.
    4. Check: \(S\) avoids the error sets \(\{8, 12\}\) and \(\{3, 12\}\), so \(|S| = 16 - 3 = 13\), and \(u, v\) agree with \(f\) and \(2 + x^2\) on those 13 points.

    6Correlated and mutual correlated agreement

    Correlated agreement is about one set shared by both words. Figure 12 shows what goes wrong when the sets differ.

    Correlated agreement says some set works. Protocols in the list regime need more: they keep track of which codeword a word is close to, and on which positions. Look again at the correlated pair of Figure 10: most folds are 3 errors away, but \(\alpha = 2, 4, 14\) give folds only 2 away. Figure 13 explains them.

    At \(\alpha = 4\) the fold agrees with \(c_0 + 4c_1\) on 14 points, including \(x = 3\), where \(u\) is wrong. A verifier that later relies on "the fold agrees with its codeword on this set" would be relying on positions where the committed words do not agree. Such \(\alpha\) must be rare, and that is the next definition.

    Definition 5 (mutual correlated agreement; ACFY24)

    The code has mutual correlated agreement (MCA) with error \(\varepsilon\) at radius \(\delta\) if for all \(u, v \in \mathbb{F}^L\): \[ \Pr_{\class{c-yellow}{\alpha}}\left[\ \exists\, \class{c-green}{S},\ |S| \ge (1-\delta)n:\ \begin{array}{l} \class{c-teal}{u + \alpha v} \text{ agrees with a codeword on } S, \text{ and} \\ (u, v) \text{ do not agree with a pair of codewords on } S \end{array} \right] \le \varepsilon. \]

    Correlated agreement asks for one good set when many folds are close. MCA asks that every set on which the fold agrees be a set on which \(u\) and \(v\) agree, for all but an \(\varepsilon\) fraction of \(\alpha\). In Figure 13 the bad \(\alpha\) are the cancellations: one per error point, at most \(\delta n\) of them, which is the shape of the unique-decoding bound \((\delta n + 1)/|\mathbb{F}|\). Up to the Johnson radius, MCA for Reed-Solomon codes is a theorem of BCHKS25, with the same \(a \cdot n / |\mathbb{F}|\) error as Theorem 2.

    MCA gives the property a list-tracking protocol needs.

    Lemma 1 (folding preserves lists)

    If the code has MCA with error \(\varepsilon\) at radius \(\delta\), then for every pair \((u, v)\), except with probability \(\varepsilon\) over \(\alpha\): \[ \mathrm{List}(\class{c-teal}{u + \alpha v},\ \delta) \;=\; \bigl\{\, c_0 + \alpha c_1 \;:\; (c_0, c_1) \in \mathrm{List}((u, v),\ \delta) \,\bigr\}, \] where \(\mathrm{List}((u, v), \delta)\) is the set of codeword pairs that agree with \((u, v)\) on one common set of size at least \((1 - \delta)n\).

    The fold's list is exactly the fold of the pair's list: no codeword appears from nowhere. Figure 14 shows the failure this rules out. It needs the toy field and the radius 8, at the edge of the Johnson range, where no guarantee holds.

    7FRI: halve, fold, repeat

    FRI (Ben-Sasson, Bentov, Horesh, Riabzev, 2018) tests proximity to \(\mathrm{RS}[\mathbb{F}, L, k]\) by turning one word into a pair of half-length words and folding them. The domain must be closed under negation: with \(L = \{1, \dots, 16\}\), each \(x\) pairs with \(-x = 17 - x\), and both have the same square.

    Split any polynomial into its even and odd parts: \[ f(x) \;=\; \underbrace{f_e(x^2)}_{\text{even powers}} \;+\; x\,\underbrace{f_o(x^2)}_{\text{odd powers}}, \qquad \deg f_e,\ \deg f_o < k/2. \] For our \(f = 3 + x + 4x^2 + x^3\): \(f_e(y) = 3 + 4y\) and \(f_o(y) = 1 + y\). Each value of \(f_e\) and \(f_o\) is read off from the pair \(f(x), f(-x)\): \[ f_e(x^2) = \frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2}, \qquad f_o(x^2) = \frac{f(x) - f(-x)}{2x}. \] The fold with challenge \(\class{c-yellow}{\alpha}\) is \(\class{c-teal}{f_1} = f_e + \alpha f_o\), a word on \(L^2\) of half the length and half the degree bound. (The division by 2 needs odd characteristic; binary-field systems such as leanVM fold with an additive variant.) Figure 16 runs FRI on the running example.

    The verifier never reads the folded words in full. In the query phase it picks a random \(x\), reads \(f(x)\) and \(f(-x)\), computes what \(f_1(x^2)\) should be, and compares with the committed \(f_1\), all the way down. Figure 17 runs it against a prover whose word is \(f\) with one wrong entry.

    Why is FRI sound? Suppose \(f\) is \(\delta\)-far. View it as the pair \((f_e, f_o)\) on \(L^2\): a pair agreeing with codewords on a set \(S' \subseteq L^2\) would make \(f\) agree with a codeword on the \(2|S'|\) square roots of \(S'\), so the pair has no \(\delta\)-correlated agreement. By Theorem 2, the true fold \(f_e + \alpha f_o\) is \(\delta\)-far except with probability \(\varepsilon\). The prover's committed \(f_1\) either equals that true fold almost everywhere, so \(f_1\) is far and the argument repeats one layer down, or it differs from the true fold on many positions. The last word is checked directly and is a codeword, so somewhere down the chain a committed word differs from the true fold of the word above on about a \(\delta\) fraction of positions or more. One query walks the whole chain \(x \to x^2 \to x^4 \to \cdots\), checking every layer, and lands on such a position with probability about \(\delta\) or more. With \(t\) queries,

    \[ \Pr[\text{accept a } \delta\text{-far word}] \;\lesssim\; \underbrace{\log_2 k \cdot \varepsilon}_{\text{an unlucky fold}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1 - \class{c-orange}{\delta})^{t}}_{\text{all queries miss}}. \]

    The first term is the proximity gap; the second is Figure 1. A protocol has \(\lambda\) bits of security when this total is at most \(2^{-\lambda}\). The larger the provable \(\delta\), the fewer queries: this is why it matters how far towards \(1 - \sqrt{\rho}\) (or beyond) the gap theorems reach.

    8WHIR: fold with a sumcheck, shrink the rate

    FRI tests proximity only. A polynomial commitment also has to prove an evaluation, and FRI-based schemes pay for that with the quotient of Figure 8. WHIR (Arnon, Chiesa, Fenzi, Yogev, 2024) folds the evaluation claim and the proximity test together, and makes each round cheaper than the last.

    WHIR views the \(k = 2^m\) coefficients of \(f\) as a multilinear polynomial \(\hat f\) in \(m\) variables (coefficient \(c_i\) goes with the monomial whose exponents are the bits of \(i\)), so FRI's fold \(f_e + \alpha f_o\) is \(\hat f\) with its first variable set to \(\alpha\). (A multilinear polynomial has degree at most 1 in each variable; the two views agree through \(p(z) = \hat f(z, z^2, z^4, \dots)\).) The object tested is a code with a constraint attached.

    Definition 6 (constrained Reed-Solomon code)

    For a weight function \(\hat w\) and a target \(\sigma \in \mathbb{F}\), \[ \mathrm{CRS}[\mathbb{F}, L, m, \hat w, \sigma] = \Bigl\{\, f \in \mathrm{RS}[\mathbb{F}, L, 2^m] \;:\; \sum_{b \in \{0,1\}^m} \hat w\bigl(\hat f(b), b\bigr) = \sigma \,\Bigr\}. \] The evaluation claim \(\hat f(z) = y\) at a point \(z \in \mathbb{F}^m\) is the case \(\hat w(Y, b) = \mathrm{eq}(z, b)\cdot Y\), \(\sigma = y\), where \(\mathrm{eq}(z, b) = \prod_i \bigl(z_i b_i + (1 - z_i)(1 - b_i)\bigr)\) is the multilinear polynomial in \(z\) that equals \([z = b]\) on \(\{0,1\}^m\), so the sum is the value \(\hat f(z)\).

    The sumcheck protocol proves a claim about a sum over \(\{0,1\}^m\) one variable at a time: in each round the prover sends the sum as a low-degree polynomial in the first remaining variable, the verifier checks it against the current claim and answers with a random challenge \(\alpha_i\), and the claim becomes one about a sum over one fewer variable, with that variable fixed to \(\alpha_i\). Fixing a variable of \(\hat f\) is exactly a fold, so the sumcheck and the proximity test advance together. Figure 18 plays one WHIR round at realistic size.

    Three ingredients of the round lean on Section 6.

    • The sumcheck fold tracks a codeword, not just a distance. The constraint is about \(\hat f\), the polynomial behind a specific list member, so after folding the verifier needs every member of the new list to be the fold of an old one. That is Lemma 1, and it is why WHIR needs MCA where FRI needs only correlated agreement.
    • The out-of-domain sample asks for the value of \(g\)'s polynomial at a random field point \(z\) outside \(L\). Distinct list members are distinct polynomials of degree less than \(k\), so at most one of them agrees with the answer, except with probability about \(\binom{|\mathrm{List}|}{2} k / |\mathbb{F}|\) (a multilinear point in \(\mathbb{F}^m\), as in leanVM, gives \(m/|\mathbb{F}|\) instead of \(k/|\mathbb{F}|\)). The list collapses to one candidate.
    • The shift queries are FRI's queries in WHIR form: they read \(t\) blocks of the old word, fold each locally (16 values for fold factor 4) and demand that the new word match at the corresponding point: \(t\) more constraints, so a far word fails one with probability about \(1 - (1-\delta)^t\).

    The query count depends on the rate through \(\delta \to 1 - \sqrt{\rho}\). FRI's rate never changes, so every layer needs the full count. WHIR's rate falls each round, so its counts fall too. Figure 19 compares them at 100 bits.

    9The real sizes

    Two numbers set a deployment's cost: the radius \(\delta\) the theorems allow, which fixes the query count, and the field size, which fixes \(\varepsilon\). Figure 20 plots the first.

    For a long time, implementations chose parameters from the conjecture that gaps hold all the way to capacity. In late 2025 Crites and Stewart showed the up-to-capacity conjectures false, and later work exhibited explicit failing lines close to capacity. The Johnson-regime theorems (BCIKS20, BCHKS25) are proven, so conservative systems, leanVM among them, stay below \(1 - \sqrt{\rho}\) and pay for it in queries.

    The second number is \(\varepsilon\). Figure 21 shows \(-\log_2 \varepsilon\) for both regimes as the field grows.

    Figure 22 replays a proximity proof end to end, naming the fact each step rests on.

    What to remember

    A verifier that reads a few entries can only distinguish codewords from words that are far from every codeword, so hash-based SNARKs prove closeness, and closeness suffices because a close word determines one polynomial or a short list.

    A random fold \(u + \alpha v\) is close for almost no \(\alpha\) or for every \(\alpha\), and the second case happens only when \(u\) and \(v\) agree with codewords on one shared set. That is the proximity gap and correlated agreement.

    Mutual correlated agreement says more: every set on which the fold agrees with the code is a set on which \(u\) and \(v\) do. It makes the fold's list the fold of the list, which protocols that track a codeword, like WHIR, require.

    FRI folds to halve the degree at constant rate. WHIR folds through a sumcheck that carries the evaluation claim, lowers the rate every round, and needs fewer queries as it goes.

    The radius sets the query count, the field sets \(\varepsilon\). The gap theorems are proven up to the Johnson radius \(1 - \sqrt{\rho}\) and fail near capacity.
