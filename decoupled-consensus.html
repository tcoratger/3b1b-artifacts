Ethereum consensus, from Gasper to EIP-8453

    Decoupled consensus

    Today every Ethereum vote does two jobs at once: it picks the head of the chain and it moves the chain towards finality. Decoupled consensus splits that vote in two. A small committee keeps the chain moving every slot, and the whole validator set finalizes on its own clock. This page shows the whole design in one picture, then opens each part.

    The 30-second version

    1. Today one attestation carries a head vote and a finality vote, so finality advances at the pace of 32-slot epochs: about 16 minutes on average.
    2. Decoupled consensus runs two protocols side by side. The available chain (Goldilocks) uses a committee of about 512 to 1,024 per slot to choose and confirm the head. The finality gadget asks every validator to vote once per round of 8 slots, and justifies then finalizes a block as soon as two thirds agree.
    3. The available chain stays live when many validators are offline; the finality gadget stays safe when the network splits. Ethereum gets both, on two layers.
    4. Expected gain: finality in under 4 minutes at today's validator count, with a path to under a minute through consolidation, one-round finality and better aggregation. Status: draft EIP-8453, a Python spec in progress, Lean proofs, a Prysm prototype and devnets.
    You need: the idea of a blockchain as blocks pointing to parents, validators who sign votes, and a fork-choice rule that picks one chain among branches.
    You will build: both layers of decoupled consensus, the reason they must be separate, how fast finality gets, and what is still open.

    1The whole design in one picture

    Figure 1 runs one round of 8 slots on a toy network of 64 validators. The top lane is the available chain: one block per slot, and a small committee that votes for the head every slot. The bottom lane is the finality gadget: the whole validator set, split into 8 cohorts, one cohort voting in each slot. Watch three tips appear on the same chain.

    The chain now has three tips. The head is the newest block and can still change. The confirmed tip comes a few seconds later and is protected by locks. The finalized tip can never change, short of a third of all stake being slashed, but comes last. Each is an ancestor of the next. The rest of the page explains each lane and the narrow interface between them.

    2Where the design comes from

    Decoupled consensus is the result of four years of research, and its documents are at different stages: research posts, a workshop, a draft EIP, a spec in progress. Figure 2 places them. A grey tag such as spec consensus-specs or workshop Cambridge, Yann names the source of every number below.

    3Why finality is slow today

    Ethereum runs Gasper: LMD-GHOST chooses the head, Casper FFG finalizes. Slots last 12 seconds and an epoch is 32 slots. Each validator attests once per epoch, in one of 32 committees, and its single attestation carries both a head vote and an FFG vote blog Upgrading Finality 1. FFG needs two rounds of two-thirds votes, one to justify a checkpoint and one to finalize it, and a round is an epoch (Figure 3).

    Why not shrink the epoch? Because the committee size ties slot time to finality time. Figure 4 shows the three choices. Smaller committees aggregate faster and allow shorter slots, but the whole set then needs more slots to speak. Larger committees speak faster, but aggregating them stretches every slot. Single-slot finality sits at the far end, with the whole set voting every slot post Francesco, ethresear.ch workshop Cambridge, Roberto.

    Coupling has two more costs. LMD-GHOST counts every validator's latest vote however old, so an attacker can withhold votes and release them later to reorg honest blocks (ex-ante reorgs) post Francesco. And LMD-GHOST cannot be proven to stay live when participation changes, so the chain's liveness under outages rests on patches rather than a proof post Luca. The available chain fixes both.

    4Layer 1: the available chain

    The top lane of Figure 1. Its job is to pick a head every slot, quickly, and to keep going whatever fraction of validators is online. It is Goldfish, a fork choice that forgets old votes, with locks added: Goldilocks, "neither too short nor too long a memory" spec Goldilocks.

    Votes that expire

    Goldfish runs GHOST, walking from the root to the child with the most votes, but at slot \(t\) it counts only the votes cast in slot \(t-1\). Figure 5 replays the attack this stops. An adversary withholds its votes for its own hidden block, then releases them late. LMD-GHOST still counts them and the honest block is reorged; Goldfish ignores them because they are a slot old.

    The slot is cut into phases of length \(\Delta\), the network delay bound: the proposer publishes at 0, committee members vote at \(\Delta\), votes received by \(2\Delta\) count as support, views freeze at \(3\Delta\). A slot is \(4\Delta\), so 3 s at 12 s slots. The next proposer packs the votes it saw into its block (view-merge), which replaces proposer boost spec Goldilocks spec EIP-8453 draft.

    Locks: memory that expires

    A one-slot memory has a weakness: a single slot of network asynchrony lets the adversary rewrite the chain post Francesco. Earlier designs added a stabilization gadget voted by the whole set. Goldilocks adds locks instead. A block is locked when at least half of one slot's whole committee votes for it (fast lock), or when \(k\) consecutive slots confirm it (slow lock). A lock steps back when half the committee votes against it, and expires after \(k\) slots (Figure 6).

    Staying alive when validators sleep

    The available chain is dynamically available: it stays safe and live as long as most of the validators that are awake are honest. Validators that go offline count against nothing post Luca. No single protocol can also stay safe under network partitions: it cannot tell a partition from low participation (the availability–finality dilemma). That is why Ethereum needs two layers, and why the available chain must stay live in outages like May 2023, when a client bug knocked out many validators yet more than 90% of slots still got a block post Luca. Figure 7 sets the participation and shows what each layer does.

    5Layer 2: the finality gadget

    The bottom lane of Figure 1. Every validator signs one finality attestation per round of 8 slots. The gadget replaces Casper FFG and lives in the beacon state; it counts in heights, not epochs spec consensus-specs.

    Heights, targets and timeouts

    At each height \(h\) the gadget has a target: the first block of that height. An attestation carries a height vote, either the target \((h, T)\) or a timeout, and optionally a finality vote for an earlier justified height. Each block applies three rules in order: finalize a pending justification that now has a quorum; else justify the target if two thirds voted for it, and move to height \(h+1\) at this very block; else, after 8 slots, move on without justifying if target votes plus timeouts reach two thirds. Figure 8 runs both paths.

    Why two thirds: accountable safety

    Two quorums of two thirds overlap in at least a third of the validators. If two conflicting blocks were ever finalized, everyone in the overlap signed two conflicting messages, and those messages are slashable: two target votes at the same height for different roots, or a vote that contradicts a finality vote at its height. Figure 9 draws the overlap. Honest validators never sign such pairs; each keeps a signing record that never expires spec consensus-specs workshop Cambridge, Roberto.

    6How the two layers talk

    The layers exchange very little, which is what makes them separable. The finality gadget hands the available chain a frame: the tree of accepted blocks above the finalized block, a fork-choice root, and which heads are viable. The available chain hands back two blocks once per round: fg_confirmed, its deepest fast-locked block, whose state gives the height vote, and its head, whose state gives the finality vote. If nothing is fast-locked, the validator does not attest (Figure 10) spec Goldilocks workshop Cambridge, Francesco.

    7Committees and timing

    In the current spec a round is 8 slots. The active validators are split into 2,048 finality committees, 256 per slot, so each validator attests once per round in its committee's slot. Separately, each slot draws a head-vote committee of 1,024 seats, sampled by effective balance spec consensus-specs. The sources disagree on that size: about 512 in the EIP spec EIP-8453 draft, and 512 seats drawn by VRF with 60% thresholds in Mikhail's open questions note Mikhail. Figure 11 lays out one round.

    8Moving a million votes

    The hard engineering problem is the finality gadget's traffic: every validator, every round, aggregated and on chain in time. A finality vote is 136 bytes of data, 248 bytes as a single gossip message; 100,000 votes need 12.5 kB of bitfield repo dc-networking. The leading proposal pipelines committees: every \(\Delta\) seconds a new committee "unit" votes for 4 s, then aggregates for 4 s, so votes and aggregates flow continuously instead of in bursts workshop Cambridge, Yann. Figure 12 is Yann's model.

    Simulations measure what the models assume. Splitting the finality votes into sub-rounds cut their p99 arrival time from about 12 s to under 2 s in a 4,000-node simulation; giving votes priority over data columns in Prysm's send queues cut vote queueing on a 5 Mbit/s uplink from 580–940 ms to about 90 ms (Figure 13). EIP-8334 bundles attestations, cutting a vote from 240 to 138 bytes workshop Cambridge, Sukun.

    9Rewards and the inactivity leak

    The current spec pays rewards only, no penalties: a validator who misses a duty forfeits that reward. Anders' sketch splits a budget of 64 between three votes, with 10 going to proposers who include them. Rewards decay with inclusion delay, so votes must land fast, and the weights keep griefing factors (what an attacker costs others per unit of its own loss) at 2 or below (Figure 14) workshop Cambridge, Anders note Anders, DC rewards.

    10How fast is finality?

    Yann's finality.watch turns all this into one number. It assumes today's coupled round takes 384 s for 1.1 million validator identities, divides by the gains of each improvement, rounds to whole slots, and adds the expected waits tool finality.watch. It is a simplified model, not a forecast. Figure 15 reproduces it, with the waiting times stakeholders asked for in the background report Finality Stakeholder Research.

    The same model breaks one transaction's wait into stages. In the "DC today" setting at 1.1 million validators, Yann's introduction shows inclusion after 6 s, confirmation 12 s later, 54 s waiting for the next round, justification 72 s after that and finalization 84 s later: 228 s in all, against about 16 minutes today (Figure 16) workshop Cambridge, Yann.

    11One-round finality

    Finality takes two rounds because the first locks and the second commits. A protocol can finalize in one round if it asks for more votes, at the cost of tolerating fewer faults. With \(t\) Byzantine and \(p\) offline validators as fractions of \(n\), one-round finality is safe and live when \(2t + 3p < n\) workshop Cambridge, Yann post Yann, One-Round Finality. Figure 17 lets you pick the threshold.

    12Ready for post-quantum signatures

    BLS signatures aggregate for free and are not quantum safe. Post-quantum signatures are about 3 KB each and must be aggregated with SNARKs. Decoupling helps twice. The head-vote committee is small enough that its post-quantum signatures can be concatenated without aggregation, so the available chain can switch first. And the finality gadget, off the critical path, gets whole seconds to build aggregation proofs post Luca workshop Cambridge, Kamil. Figure 18 runs Kamil's aggregation-time model.

    13Status and open questions

    The design converged at the Cambridge workshop of 13–15 October 2026 and is a headliner candidate for the I* fork blog Upgrading Finality 1. Where things stand:

    • EIP-8453 is a draft pull request; its stated goals are a foundation for post-quantum signatures, stronger fork choice, and at least four times faster finality spec EIP-8453 draft.
    • Spec: the finality gadget, committees, rewards and inactivity are in consensus-specs under _features/eip8453; fork choice and networking are still TODO spec consensus-specs.
    • Proofs: two Lean 4 developments prove accountable safety, finality liveness after synchrony returns, and confirmation properties of the available chain, under a one-third adversary proof Lean.
    • Code: a Prysm branch implements Goldilocks fork choice and the finality gadget on a devnet; benchmarks run on Shadow and on 1,000-node devnets code Prysm repo dc-networking.

    14What to remember

    • One vote used to do two jobs; decoupled consensus splits it into a head vote by a small committee every slot and a finality vote by everyone once per round.
    • The available chain (Goldilocks) forgets votes after one slot, which kills ex-ante reorgs, and adds expiring locks for fast confirmation. It stays live when validators sleep.
    • The finality gadget counts heights, justifies at two thirds and finalizes with a second two-thirds quorum, and is accountably safe: conflicting finality costs a third of all stake.
    • The two exchange only a frame and two blocks per round, so finality traffic never slows the fork choice.
    • Finality drops from about 16 minutes to under 4 at today's size; consolidation, bundling, one-round finality and post-quantum aggregation decide how far below a minute it can go.
