EIP draft · ethereum/EIPs #12604 · from zero

    Post-quantum PeerDAS

    How Ethereum can keep checking blob data it never downloads after elliptic curves stop being safe: hash each blob into a Merkle tree, prove with a STARK that the tree holds a valid code, and let blobs come in nine sizes instead of one.

    You need: what a hash function is (a fixed-size output, and no known way to find two inputs with the same output), and the fact that Ethereum blocks can carry blobs of rollup data.
    You will build: the full journey of one sampled cell, from a rollup's transaction to a phone that checks it with seven hashes; the fee rule for variable-size blobs; and the open questions the authors are debating.

    1Checking data you never download

    A rollup publishes its transactions on Ethereum as a blob: a byte string attached to a block, kept by the network for about 18 days, and never read by the EVM. Before a node accepts a block, it has to know that every blob in it was actually published. If the block producer withheld part of a blob, the rollup's users could not rebuild their own state, even though the block looks valid.

    Downloading every blob answers the question, at a bandwidth cost that grows with every blob added to the block. PeerDAS (EIP-7594), live since the Fusaka upgrade, answers it by sampling: each node downloads a few small pieces chosen at random, and checks each piece against a short commitment. Figure 1 plays the whole story of this EIP in six beats.

    The last two beats are the reason the draft exists. Every proof PeerDAS uses today is a KZG opening, and KZG is secure only while discrete logarithms on an elliptic curve are hard to compute. Shor's algorithm computes them on a large enough quantum computer. The draft swaps each KZG opening for a Merkle authentication path, whose security rests on Keccak-256 alone, and changes as little else as it can. It also uses the occasion to let blobs come in sizes other than one.

    The page climbs to that design one rung at a time. Section 2 cuts a blob into cells and columns. Section 3 shows why half of an encoded blob is enough. Section 4 says what a sampled cell must prove and why KZG has to go. Section 5 builds the Merkle tree, Section 6 shows what it cannot prove and the STARK that fills the gap, Section 7 adds variable sizes and fees, Section 8 follows a transaction into a block, Section 9 covers networking, and Section 10 maps the open debate.

    2Blobs, cells and columns

    The draft fixes one number above all others: every blob is cut into exactly 128 pieces.

    Definition 1 (blob, cell, column)

    A blob is a byte string of length \(\class{c-orange}{L}\) that is a codeword of a rate-½ Reed–Solomon code (Definition 2). It is cut into 128 cells of \(\class{c-orange}{L}/128\) bytes each: cell \(i\) is bytes \(i\cdot \class{c-orange}{L}/128\) up to \((i+1)\cdot \class{c-orange}{L}/128 - 1\). Column \(i\) of a block is the list made of cell \(i\) of every blob in the block, in block order.

    Each row is a blob of 128 cells. A column is the same position taken from every row: the highlighted cells form column 2.

    Our running example is a blob we call \(B\). It has \(\class{c-orange}{L} = 2048\) bytes, the smallest size the draft allows, so each cell is 16 bytes. Its first 64 cells hold rollup data, here the text Rollup batch 42: 3 transfers, 1 swap, state root follows. repeated; its last 64 cells hold redundancy computed from the first half, built in Section 3. Figure 2 lays out all 128 cells. Cell 21, which we follow for the rest of the page, holds the 16 bytes ot follows. Roll.

    PeerDAS hands out columns, not blobs. A full node custodies (stores and serves) at least 4 of the 128 columns and samples at least 8 per slot; a node running validators custodies more, in proportion to their stake. Since column 21 contains cell 21 of every blob, a node assigned column 21 holds 1/128 of each blob in the block, and nothing more.

    Under the draft, blobs may have different lengths, yet every blob still has exactly 128 cells. A long blob has wide cells and a short blob narrow ones. Figure 3 shows what that does to a block: the rows change thickness, and the columns stay where they were.

    3Why half the data is enough

    Sampling raw data does not work. Suppose a producer publishes 127 of 128 columns and hides one. A node that samples 8 distinct columns misses the hidden one with probability \(120/128 \approx 94\%\), and a single hidden column can contain the one transaction that matters. To make sampling meaningful, the data must be stretched so that hiding anything forces the producer to hide a lot.

    Definition 2 (Reed–Solomon codeword of rate ½)

    Fix a field \(\mathbb{F}\), a number \(k\), and \(2k\) distinct points \(x_0, \dots, x_{2k-1}\) of \(\mathbb{F}\). A word \((y_0, \dots, y_{2k-1})\) is a codeword if some polynomial \(f\) of degree less than \(k\) satisfies \(f(x_j) = y_j\) for every \(j\). The rate \(k / 2k = 1/2\) is the share of the word that carries data.

    Theorem 1 (any half determines the whole)

    Any \(k\) entries of a codeword determine the other \(k\).

    Proof. Let \(f\) and \(g\) both have degree less than \(k\) and agree on \(k\) points. Then \(f - g\) has degree less than \(k\) and \(k\) roots. A nonzero polynomial of degree less than \(k\) has fewer than \(k\) roots, so \(f - g = 0\). The \(k\) known entries therefore pin down \(f\), and \(f\) gives every other entry. ∎

    Figure 4 runs the smallest useful case: the field \(\mathbb{F}_{17}\) of integers modulo 17, \(k = 4\), and points \(0, 1, \dots, 7\). The data is \((3, 1, 4, 1)\), placed at \(x = 0, 1, 2, 3\). The unique \(f\) of degree below 4 through those points continues to \(f(4), \dots, f(7) = 15, 1, 16, 15\). Erase columns by tapping them and watch what survives.

    Example 1 (recovering \(f(4)\) from the data half)

    Lagrange interpolation writes \(f(4) = \sum_{i=0}^{3} y_i\,\ell_i(4)\), where \(\ell_i(4) = \prod_{j \ne i} \frac{4 - j}{i - j}\) is 1 at \(x = i\) and 0 at the other three data points.

    1. \(\ell_0(4) = \frac{3\cdot 2\cdot 1}{(-1)(-2)(-3)} = -1\),
      \(\ell_1(4) = \frac{4\cdot 2\cdot 1}{1\cdot(-1)(-2)} = 4\),
      \(\ell_2(4) = \frac{4\cdot 3\cdot 1}{2\cdot 1\cdot(-1)} = -6\),
      \(\ell_3(4) = \frac{4\cdot 3\cdot 2}{3\cdot 2\cdot 1} = 4\).
    2. \(f(4) = 3(-1) + 1(4)\) \({}+ 4(-6) + 1(4)\) \({}= -19\).
    3. Reduce modulo 17: \(-19 + 2\cdot 17 = 15\). This is the value in column 4 of Figure 4.

    The real code works the same way at a larger size. The draft calls it an additive Reed–Solomon code and leaves the exact field to the Lean Ethereum specifications. For blob \(B\) this page uses the binary field \(\mathbb{F}_{2^{16}}\): each 2-byte pair is one field element, the 2048 bytes are \(1024\) elements, \(k = 512\), and the evaluation points \(0, 1, \dots, 1023\) form an additive subgroup of the field. The data sits at points \(0\) to \(511\) (cells 0 to 63); cells 64 to 127 are \(f(512), \dots, f(1023)\). Each cell holds 8 field elements, so any 64 columns supply 512 points, which is exactly \(k\). Figure 5 rebuilds \(B\) from whatever columns you leave it.

    Theorem 1 turns sampling into a strong test. To make blob data unrecoverable, a producer must withhold at least 65 of the 128 columns, leaving at most 63. A node that asks for \(s\) distinct random columns and gets every answer has been lucky.

    Theorem 2 (sampling catches withholding)

    If at most 63 columns are available, a node that samples \(s\) distinct columns uniformly at random receives all \(s\) with probability at most

    \[ \Pr[\text{all answered}] = \frac{\binom{63}{s}}{\binom{128}{s}} \;<\; \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{s}. \]

    Proof. There are \(\binom{128}{s}\) equally likely sample sets, and \(\binom{63}{s}\) of them lie inside the available columns. Each factor of the ratio \(\frac{63 - j}{128 - j}\) is below \(\tfrac12\). ∎

    For \(s = 8\) the bound is \(0.00271\), about 1 in 369. Figure 6 lets you change \(s\).

    4What a sampled cell must prove

    A sample is answered by a peer, and peers can lie. A node that asks for cell 21 of blob \(B\) needs to know that the 16 bytes it got are the 16 bytes the transaction committed to, and not bytes the peer made up. So each blob has a short commitment \(\class{c-teal}{C}\), signed into its transaction, and each cell travels with a proof that the cell at position \(i\) of the blob behind \(\class{c-teal}{C}\) has exactly these bytes. A commitment scheme for PeerDAS has two jobs.

    • Binding. Nobody can produce valid proofs for two different values of the same cell.
    • Codeword. The committed bytes form a codeword. Without this, two nodes rebuilding from two different halves could get two different blobs; Section 6 shows how.

    KZG, the scheme PeerDAS uses today, does both at once. It reads the blob as the evaluations of a polynomial \(f\) of degree below \(k\), and commits to \(f\) itself. A commitment to a polynomial of degree below \(k\) makes the evaluations a codeword for free, by Definition 2.

    Why KZG breaks

    A trusted-setup ceremony published the points \([\tau^j]G\) for a secret scalar \(\tau\) nobody knows. The commitment is \(\class{c-purple}{C} = [f(\tau)]G\), and the proof that \(f(z) = y\) is

    \[ \class{c-purple}{\pi} = \Big[\tfrac{f(\tau) - y}{\tau - z}\Big]G, \]

    checked by one pairing equation. Whoever knows \(\tau\) can open \(\class{c-purple}{C}\) to any value \(\class{c-red}{y'}\) without knowing \(f\):

    \[ \class{c-red}{\pi'} = \underbrace{(\tau - z)^{-1}}_{\text{needs }\tau}\cdot\big(\underbrace{\class{c-purple}{C}}_{\text{public}} - \class{c-red}{y'}\cdot G\big). \]

    Shor's algorithm recovers \(\tau\) from the public point \([\tau]G\). Binding is then gone: a peer can serve forged cells that pass verification.

    The replacement this draft picks is a Merkle tree over the cells. It costs more bytes per sample, as Figure 7 shows, but its only assumption is that Keccak-256 is collision resistant, and the best known quantum attack on that (Grover-style search) only shrinks the security margin instead of breaking it.

    5A Merkle tree over one blob

    A Merkle tree turns 128 cells into one 32-byte value, in such a way that any single cell can later be checked against that value with 7 hashes.

    Definition 3 (blob Merkle root)

    Leaf \(i\) is \(\ell_i = \mathrm{keccak256}(\class{c-blue}{\text{cell}_i})\). Each node above the leaves is \(\mathrm{keccak256}(\text{left} \,\|\, \text{right})\), the hash of its two children's 32-byte values joined into 64 bytes. With 128 leaves the tree has \(\log_2 128 = 7\) levels above the leaves (the draft's MERKLE_DEPTH) and \(64 + 32 + 16 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 127\) internal nodes. The top node is the blob Merkle root.

    Figure 8 builds the tree for \(B\) one level per beat, with the real Keccak-256 values. The root is c151d860…7a30b291.

    Definition 4 (authentication path)

    The authentication path of cell \(i\) is the list of the 7 siblings met while walking from leaf \(i\) up to the root: at each level, the other child of the current node's parent. Bit \(d\) of \(i\) (counting from the least significant) says whether the current node is a left child (bit 0) or a right child (bit 1) at level \(d\).

    Figure 9 draws the path for any cell. Leave it on cell 21: in binary \(21 = 0010101_2\), so reading from the bottom the walk goes right, left, right, left, right, left, left.

    A node that receives cell 21 and its path recomputes the root and compares. The draft's function is short enough to trace line by line, which Figure 10 does with the real values.

    Theorem 3 (binding)

    If two different cells both verify at position \(i\) against the same root, then the two computations contain a Keccak-256 collision.

    Proof. Write the two chains of values \(v_0, \dots, v_7\) and \(v'_0, \dots, v'_7\), where \(v_0 = \mathrm{keccak256}(\text{cell})\) and \(v_7 = v'_7 = \) root. Take the smallest \(d\) with \(v_{d} = v'_{d}\). If \(d = 0\), the two different cells hash to the same leaf: a collision. If \(d \ge 1\), the inputs at level \(d\) are \(v_{d-1}\) joined with a sibling and \(v'_{d-1}\) joined with a sibling; they differ because \(v_{d-1} \ne v'_{d-1}\), yet they hash to the same value: a collision. ∎

    Figure 11 flips a single bit of cell 21, the letter o (0x6f) becoming n (0x6e), and keeps the honest path. Every value of the chain changes, and the last one is no longer the root.

    6What a Merkle root cannot promise

    Section 4 asked two jobs of a commitment. The Merkle root does the first. It does not do the second: a Merkle tree commits to any bytes, codeword or not. Figure 12 shows the damage on the code of Figure 4. The producer commits to the word \((3, 1, 4, 1, 15, 1, 16, \class{c-red}{2})\), which differs from the codeword in its last entry.

    Checking the codeword property directly means reading the whole blob, which is what sampling exists to avoid. The draft instead attaches a STARK: a proof, far smaller than the blobs and fast to verify, that a stated program ran to completion on some inputs. A STARK uses only hash functions, so it keeps the post-quantum assumption the same as the Merkle tree's. The program is called the guest program, and the draft's version is LeanDA.

    Definition 5 (LeanDA guest program)

    The public input is a list of blob commitments, each a pair \((\class{c-teal}{\text{root}}, \class{c-orange}{L})\). The private input is the list of blobs. The program accepts if, for every blob in order, (1) its length \(\class{c-orange}{L}\) is allowed, (2) its Merkle root equals the committed root, and (3) its bytes satisfy the Reed–Solomon relation, through a check the draft has not yet specified (see "Which Reed–Solomon check?" in this section).

    The STARK makes the statement "some private input makes this program accept" checkable without the private input. Anyone holding the proof and the public commitments learns that each committed root is the root of a valid codeword of the committed length, and downloads no blob to learn it. Figure 13 shows the three checks on blob \(B\), and where each piece lives: the blob and its tree inside the prover, the commitment and the proof in public.

    Figure 14 runs the same program as code, on a batch of two blobs: \(B\) and a second blob \(C\) of 4096 bytes.

    The arrangement has a clean split between what is public and what is not, drawn in Figure 15. Each blob and its tree stay with the prover. Only two kinds of object cross to the verifier: one commitment per blob, and one proof for the batch.

    The draft is explicit about what the proof does not cover. The STARK shows correct encoding and correct commitments; it says nothing about whether the cells were published. Availability still comes from sampling (Theorem 2) and from nodes custodying columns. The two pieces together give PeerDAS what KZG used to give it.

    Which Reed–Solomon check?

    The draft does not yet define verify_reed_solomon_relation (line 5 of Figure 14), and the EIP has to. The exact check recomputes the redundancy half from the data half, as in Figure 5. The expected one is cheaper: multiply the blob entry by entry with a random vector \(\class{c-green}{r}\), add up, and check that the sum is zero.

    Definition 6 (dual check)

    Take the weights \(v_i = 1/\prod_{j \ne i}(x_i - x_j)\) and a random polynomial \(\class{c-green}{g}\) of degree below \(k\), and set \(\class{c-green}{r_i} = v_i\,\class{c-green}{g}(x_i)\). The check accepts a word \(\class{c-blue}{w}\) when \(\langle \class{c-green}{r}, \class{c-blue}{w}\rangle = \sum_i \class{c-green}{r_i}\,\class{c-blue}{w_i} = 0\).

    Theorem 4

    A codeword always passes. Any other word passes with probability \(1/|\mathbb{F}|\).

    Why

    Codeword. If \(w_i = f(x_i)\) with \(\deg f < k\), the sum is \(\sum_i v_i\,h(x_i)\) for \(h = f\class{c-green}{g}\) of degree at most \(2k-2\). By Lagrange's formula that sum is the \(x^{2k-1}\) coefficient of \(h\), which is 0.

    Other words. \(\class{c-green}{g} \mapsto \langle r, w\rangle\) is a linear map, and it is not zero (only codewords are orthogonal to every \(r\)). A nonzero linear map hits 0 for a fraction \(1/|\mathbb{F}|\) of its inputs. ∎

    Figure 16 runs the check on the toy code. The corrupted word of Figure 12 passes 1 challenge in 17 (4913 of the 83 521, counted one by one). A real check makes this negligible with a larger field or repeated checks.

    The third button of Figure 16 shows why the challenge must come after the blob is fixed: a prover who knows \(\class{c-green}{r}\) in advance edits one entry and passes. So \(\class{c-green}{r}\) is derived from the commitments, by a Fiat–Shamir hash of the Merkle roots. Figure 17 shows where each step runs.

    The hash has to run outside the guest. The security proof treats the Fiat–Shamir hash as a random oracle, and there is no known way to prove security when a random oracle is evaluated inside the proved computation; the Merkle hash inside the guest only needs collision resistance. Expanding the seed into the full vector is deterministic, so it can run inside the guest, which keeps that work out of the EIP-8288 recursion circuit.

    Variable sizes add one cost: blobs of different lengths need vectors of different lengths, where one fixed size could share a single vector across all blobs. The EIP can still avoid naming a field by specifying the check abstractly, as a code checker in the sense of the formal-verification post in Further reading.

    7Blobs of nine sizes

    Before this draft, a blob had one size. A rollup with 3 KB to post paid for a whole blob or waited until it had more data. The draft allows any length \(\class{c-orange}{L}\) that is a power of two between 2048 and 524 288 bytes. That is nine sizes, \(2^{11}\) to \(2^{19}\):

    \[ \texttt{is\_valid\_blob\_byte\_length}(\class{c-orange}{L}) \iff 2^{11} \le \class{c-orange}{L} \le 2^{19} \text{ and } \class{c-orange}{L} \text{ is a power of two.} \]

    (The draft also requires \(L\) to be divisible by 128, which every power of two in this range already is.) The number of cells is always 128, so changing \(L\) changes only the cell width \(L/128\), from 16 to 4096 bytes. The tree above the cells keeps its shape, so the Merkle path stays 7 hashes. Figure 18 shows which parts move with \(\class{c-orange}{L}\) and which do not.

    Figure 19 puts the nine sizes side by side. The path dominates the sample at small sizes: 93% of what a node downloads to check a 2048-byte blob is path. This is the fixed per-sample cost that Section 10 comes back to.

    Paying for blob space

    Blob gas is the unit the blob fee market prices. EIP-4844 charges a flat \(2^{17} = 131\,072\) blob gas per blob. The draft charges per byte, plus a fixed amount for the tree:

    \[ \texttt{blob\_gas} = \underbrace{\class{c-orange}{L}}_{\text{blob bytes}} + \underbrace{127 \times 32}_{\text{internal nodes} \,=\, 4064}. \]

    The 4064 bytes are the 127 internal nodes of the tree (Definition 3), each 32 bytes. The draft calls this an accounting convention rather than a count of bytes sent over the wire. For blob \(B\): \(2048 + 4064 = 6112\) blob gas, against \(131\,072\) for a fixed-size blob today, 21 times less. Figure 20 shows the gas paid per blob byte.

    The fee schedule has to move to the new unit at the fork. The draft defines a legacy-equivalent blob as \(131\,072 + 4064 = 135\,136\) blob gas (FULL_BLOB_GAS), multiplies the target and the maximum blob counts by it, and rescales the update fraction so the base fee reacts to one legacy-equivalent blob exactly as it does today.

    Example 2 (converting a schedule)

    Take the schedule target 14, max 21, update fraction 11 684 671 (the BPO2 entry of EIP-7892). Figure 21 draws each step.

    1. ① target_blob_gas \(= 14 \times 135\,136 = 1\,891\,904\).
    2. ② max_blob_gas \(= 21 \times 135\,136 = 2\,837\,856\).
    3. ③ base_fee_update_fraction is \(11\,684\,671 \times 135\,136\) \({}/\, 131\,072\), rounded up: \(12\,046\,965\). The ratio to one blob, 0.01122, is unchanged.
    4. ④ Most blobs a block can hold: \(2\,837\,856 / 6112 = 464.3\), so \(464\), all of size 2048.

    One point in the draft is still loose. Definition 1 calls the blob the whole codeword, data plus redundancy, while FULL_BLOB_GAS treats 131 072 bytes as a legacy-sized blob, and 131 072 bytes is what EIP-4844 counts as the data of one blob (its extended form is twice that). Read literally, a 131 072-byte blob under this draft carries 65 536 bytes of rollup data. The text will need to say which reading it means.

    The versioned hash

    Contracts see blobs through the BLOBHASH opcode, which returns a 32-byte versioned hash. The draft derives it from the commitment pair, binding both the root and the length:

    \[ \texttt{versioned\_hash} = \mathtt{0x02} \,\|\, \mathrm{keccak256}\big(\class{c-teal}{\text{root}} \,\|\, \mathrm{le64}(\class{c-orange}{L})\big)[1{:}32]. \]

    The first byte is the version, 0x02 for post-quantum blobs (0x01 is KZG); the other 31 are the hash with its first byte dropped. Figure 22 computes it for \(B\).

    8From transaction to block

    Two other drafts carry the blob into a block. EIP-8141 defines frame transactions: a transaction made of a list of frames, each a contract call, with some frames validating the transaction and paying for gas and the others doing the work. EIP-8288 adds a frame mode for dependencies: triples \((\text{scheme}, \text{data\_hash}, \text{vk\_hash})\), each saying "this transaction is valid only if a proof exists for the statement data_hash under the verification key vk_hash". The proofs are not stored in the transaction. Instead, every block carries one recursive STARK that proves all dependencies of all its transactions at once.

    This draft plugs LeanDA into that machinery. A blob-carrying frame transaction replaces the old list of versioned hashes by a signed list blob_commitments of \((\class{c-teal}{\text{root}}, \class{c-orange}{L})\) pairs, at most 6 blobs and \(786\,432\) bytes in total, and must declare one dependency with scheme LEANDA_SCHEME = 0x12 whose data_hash is the hash of those commitments. Figure 23 shows the layout for a transaction carrying \(B\) and \(C\).

    The data_hash is Keccak-256 of the tag LEANDA_PUBLIC_INPUT_V1, the number of commitments as 8 little-endian bytes, then each root followed by its length. For \(B\) and \(C\) that is a 110-byte string whose hash is 5670467c…93925ec8. Figure 24 follows the transaction from the user to the nodes that sample it.

    The order of events matters for latency. Under EIP-8288, proving happens in the pool, spread over whoever runs provers, before any builder picks the transaction. The builder's job at block time is to fold proofs that already exist into the block's recursive STARK. Proving is therefore already off the builder's critical path, so the draft needs no separate scheme for delaying proofs.

    9Rows, not columns

    The draft builds one Merkle tree per blob, over the blob's own 128 cells: a row tree. An alternative would be one tree per column, over cell \(i\) of every blob in the block. The draft rejects it, and Figure 25 shows why. A column tree depends on which blobs end up in the block and in what order, which is known only when the block is built. A row tree depends on nothing but its blob.

    With row trees, anyone who holds a blob can derive any cell and its path at once and start serving it, long before the block is final. Figure 26 shows the timing and the reason.

    Row trees also match how PeerDAS samples. The code extends each blob along its own row, so the unit a node samples is a column: cell \(i\) of every blob. With one root per blob, a column therefore carries one path per cell, in the place where each cell carries a KZG proof today. Nothing else in sampling has to change, and that is the reason for the choice: cell-level deltas and the sparse blob pool keep working as they are.

    The message that carries a column keeps its name, DataColumnSidecar. For column \(i\) it lists, blob by blob, cell \(i\) and that cell's 7-hash path; the KZG proofs are gone. A receiver runs two checks on each entry: the path must lead from the cell to that blob's root (compute_root_from_path), and the cell must be \(L/128\) bytes long for that blob's \(L\). Figure 27 builds the sidecar for column 21 of a block with four blobs of different sizes.

    In the execution-layer blob pool (EIP-8070's sparse blobpool, here on eth/73), a node asks a peer for the cells it wants with a 128-bit cell_mask, one bit per cell. The peer replies with each requested cell and its path, and the node checks every path against the root. One case is cheaper: when the mask asks for all 128 cells, the peer sends no paths, because the node can rebuild the whole tree from the cells and compare a single root. Figure 28 shows both. The Engine API applies the same rule in engine_getBlobsV5.

    One gap remains in the networking part: the draft lists sidecar gossip and request rules as unchanged, but never says how the consensus-layer request DataColumnSidecarsByRoot changes.

    10What is still open

    The draft is an early proposal, and the first review raised four questions beyond the Reed–Solomon check of Section 6. Each is stated here as an argument.

    Proving cost grows with the largest blob

    The Reed–Solomon check of Section 6 multiplies each blob by a random vector \(r\), and \(r\) must be as long as the largest blob in the batch; equal blobs share one. A simple cost model is therefore: total bytes plus the length of the largest blob. Sixteen blobs of 32 KiB cost \(512 + 32 = 544\); one blob of 512 KiB, the same data, costs \(512 + 512 = 1024\), about twice as much, which matches the rough estimate given in review. Figure 29 draws both.

    The case for small blobs anyway: the missing feature is paying for the data you actually need, and a sender who needs more than one blob can already include up to 6 per transaction. Blob packing, several senders sharing one blob, is the other way to get there; the draft bets on variable sizes.

    Variable sizes and a two-dimensional code

    The draft is one-dimensional: each blob is extended along its own row, and a column is just one slice of each row, with no code of its own. A two-dimensional design also extends down the columns, so each column is a codeword too. That works only when every row has the same cell size: a column cannot be one codeword over 16-byte and 4096-byte symbols at once (Figure 30). Variable sizes would then need re-coding, and if data can be re-coded, calldata could be re-coded instead. A second cost is the fixed path of Figure 19: every sample pays one, 256 to 400 bytes for a Merkle branch, which limits how small a useful sample can be.

    The draft stays 1D on purpose, so that sampling, custody and dissemination stay as they are. 2D is itself a form of re-coding: some process must compute the new rows, and no re-coding mechanism yet avoids putting the whole burden on the builder. The candidates are random-linear-combination recoding and in-network coding, where the builder sends out part of the data and the network computes the rest.

    How much bandwidth is light?

    The case for 2D is scale. Around 8 MB/s of blob data is the usual line between "L1-scale", where 1D and in-circuit hashing are fine, and "super-scale", where neither is. A node that samples 8 of 128 columns downloads 8/128 of that, \(0.5\) MB/s, and every node pays it, phones and browser extensions included. More columns shrink that share (Figure 31).

    Should blobs exist at all?

    The deepest question is whether blobs should stay a separate kind of data.

    • For merging them into calldata: once a zkEVM proves execution, nobody re-executes; the 18-day blob lifetime was a short-term compromise, not a principle; and one way to publish data is simpler than two.
    • For keeping them apart: blob data is static, opaque and never executed. A thousand blobs in calldata would all be hashed inside the zkEVM on the critical path, losing the distributed, pipelined proving that per-blob commitments allow. At super-scale both sides accept this point.

    It helps to separate three guarantees: what each node learns from sampling, what validators attest about availability in consensus, and how light nodes' samples help rescue and rebuild data. The draft changes only the first. KZG is the one part of the data path that a quantum computer breaks, and the replacement keeps everything else fixed.

    What to remember

    A blob is a rate-½ Reed–Solomon codeword cut into 128 cells, and any 64 columns rebuild it. Sampling a few columns at random catches a producer who hides enough to matter.

    Each sampled cell must prove it belongs to its blob. KZG did this with 48 bytes, and a quantum computer can forge those proofs. The draft uses a Merkle path of 7 Keccak-256 hashes, 224 bytes, which stays binding as long as Keccak-256 is collision resistant.

    A Merkle root accepts any bytes, so a LeanDA STARK proves that each committed root is the root of a valid codeword of the committed length. EIP-8288 folds those proofs into one recursive STARK per block. The draft does not yet say which Reed–Solomon check the guest runs; the likely one is a dual inner product, whose randomness must come from a Fiat–Shamir hash of the roots computed outside the guest and passed in as a public input.

    Blobs come in nine power-of-two sizes from 2 KiB to 512 KiB, always as 128 cells. Gas is the length plus 4064, so small blobs pay a fixed tree charge, and the per-sample path dominates their bandwidth.

    The open questions are the exact Reed–Solomon relation, proving cost for uneven batches, compatibility with a 2D code, how light sampling can get, and whether blobs should remain separate from calldata.

    Further reading

    1. The draft itself: ethereum/EIPs #12604, "Post-Quantum PeerDAS and Variable-Size Blobs". Specification, rationale and backwards compatibility.
    2. EIP-7594: PeerDAS. Columns, custody and sampling as they stand today, with KZG cell proofs.
    3. EIP-4844: Shard Blob Transactions. Blobs, versioned hashes, and the blob fee market the draft converts.
    4. EIP-8288 (in-mempool signature and proof aggregation) and EIP-8141 (frame transactions), in the EIPs repository. Dependencies and the per-block recursive STARK.
    5. Csaba Kiraly, "PQ-DAS off the critical path". An alternative with optimistic encoding and delayed proofs, to compare with the in-pool proving of Section 8.
    6. Dankrad Feist, "KZG polynomial commitments". The scheme being replaced, with the opening equation of Section 4.
    7. Benedikt Wagner, "Formally verified security for PQ DAS (LeanDA)" and "LeanDA: design and benchmark", ethresear.ch. The security argument behind the Fiat–Shamir requirement of Section 6, and the LeanDA construction.
    8. Eli Ben-Sasson, Iddo Bentov, Yinon Horesh, Michael Riabzev, "Scalable, transparent, and post-quantum secure computational integrity" (2018). The STARK paper.
