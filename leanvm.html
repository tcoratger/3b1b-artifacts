Hash-based proofs from zero

    leanVM, from A to Z

    A six-instruction machine over binary fields, and the proof system that convinces anyone it ran correctly using nothing but Keccak and polynomials. Built for one job: aggregating Ethereum's post-quantum signatures and blobs.

    You need: arithmetic modulo 2 (XOR), polynomials and their roots, what a hash function and a digital signature are, and a Merkle tree.
    You will build: every step of a leanVM proof, from the machine's instruction set to the last Merkle query, with the reason each check exists and what would break without it.
    Source: the leanVM specification (doc/leanvm) and the Rust implementation on the main branch of leanEthereum/leanVM. Section and annex numbers below refer to that specification.

    1Why Ethereum wants a proof machine

    Ethereum relies on three tools built on elliptic curves. Its validators sign with BLS, its users sign transactions with ECDSA, and blobs, the large data packets that rollups publish, are committed with KZG polynomial commitments. Shor's algorithm, run on a large enough quantum computer, computes discrete logarithms on any elliptic curve, so all three would fall together. Hash functions do not have this weakness: the best known quantum attack on a hash is a generic search.

    Figure 1 shows the replacement plan and the problem it creates. Each elliptic-curve tool has a hash-based substitute, but the substitutes are large and, unlike BLS, they do not aggregate. A thousand BLS signatures on one message combine into one 96-byte signature; a thousand leanXMSS signatures are a thousand times 2404 bytes.

    What does "a proof certifies many signatures" mean? A prover runs a program, the guest, that verifies every signature. It then produces a short string, the proof, from which anyone can check that the guest ran to completion on those inputs, in far less time than re-running it. A system that does this for arbitrary programs is a verifiable virtual machine, often called a zkVM. leanVM is one, with two qualifications: its proofs are not zero knowledge (they hide nothing, and nothing here needs hiding), and its only cryptographic assumption is the hash function Keccak-256, Ethereum's own.

    The guest that leanVM proves can itself verify leanVM proofs. So proofs nest: a node verifies two child proofs and a batch of raw signatures and emits one proof for all of it. That recursion is how a network of aggregators shrinks the signatures of one slot (Ethereum's 12-second block interval) to one proof.

    A proof system is a long pipeline. Figure 2 is the map of this page. Each box is a section; the band at the bottom, Fiat-Shamir, runs under all of them and turns the conversation between prover and verifier into a single string.

    The page builds that map from its parts. Sections 2 to 4 build the objects: the fields, the machine and the handful of polynomial facts every later step uses. Sections 5 to 8 turn an execution into tables and prove the tables consistent. Sections 9 and 10 commit to the tables and open them. Sections 11 to 13 handle the hash: Keccak inside the machine, ring switching and Fiat-Shamir. Section 14 replays the whole protocol at once, Sections 15 and 16 show what runs on top (the compiler's tricks, the signature schemes, data availability and recursion), and Section 17 collects the soundness argument.

    2Two binary fields

    Every number in leanVM is an element of a binary field: a field whose elements are polynomials with coefficients in \(\{0, 1\}\), added and multiplied modulo a fixed irreducible polynomial. The choice is driven by the hash. Keccak is built from XOR, AND and bit rotations; in a binary field XOR is addition, so the hash's linear steps cost nothing, and the AND of two bits is their product.

    Definition 1 (the fields K and E)

    \[ \class{c-blue}{K} = \mathbb{F}_2[x]\,/\,(x^{64}+x^4+x^3+x+1), \qquad E = K[y]\,/\,(y^3+y+1), \qquad |E| = 2^{192}. \]

    An element of \(K\) is a polynomial in \(x\) of degree below 64, stored as a 64-bit word with the coefficient of \(x^i\) in bit \(i\). In hexadecimal, \(\mathtt{2}\) is \(x\), \(\mathtt{3}\) is \(x+1\) and \(\mathtt{1b}\) is \(x^4+x^3+x+1\). An element of \(E\) is three elements of \(K\): its coordinates on \(1, y, y^2\).

    Addition in \(K\) adds coefficients modulo 2: bitwise XOR, with no carries. Multiplication multiplies the polynomials without carries (a carry-less product, up to 127 bits) and then reduces modulo \(x^{64}+x^4+x^3+x+1\): every power \(x^{64+j}\) is replaced by \(x^{j+4}+x^{j+3}+x^{j+1}+x^{j}\). Figure 3 does both on any two words you type.

    Counting in the exponent

    A machine needs addresses: memory word 0, word 1, word 2. A binary field has no "plus one" that walks through all elements, because \(1+1 = 0\). It has another walk. The element \(\class{c-yellow}{g} = x\) generates the multiplicative group \(K^\times\): its powers \(g^0, g^1, \dots, g^{2^{64}-2}\) are all distinct and cover every nonzero element. leanVM therefore writes the index \(i\) as the element \(g^i\), and "next address" is one multiplication by \(g\). Figure 4 walks the first steps.

    This is the convention everywhere in leanVM, and Section 15 shows how much it buys: the program counter, the frame pointer, memory addresses, loop counters and lookup counters are all powers of \(g\). An offset \(o = g^j\) applied to a frame pointer \(\mathbf{fp} = g^i\) gives the address \(\mathbf{fp}\cdot o = g^{i+j}\): pointer arithmetic is one field multiplication.

    The extension E, and why it exists

    \(K\) has \(2^{64}\) elements. Section 4 shows that the verifier's checks are fooled with probability around \(d/|\mathbb{F}|\) for the field \(\mathbb{F}\) the challenges come from, with \(d\) a small degree. With \(|\mathbb{F}| = 2^{64}\) that is far from the 128 bits of security the system targets, so challenges, and memory words, live in the degree-3 extension \(E\) of size \(2^{192}\). An element of \(E\) is \(c_0 + c_1 y + c_2 y^2\) with \(c_i \in K\), and \(y^3 = y + 1\) reduces products:

    \[ (\textstyle\sum_i a_i y^i)(\sum_j b_j y^j) = \underbrace{p_0 + p_1 y + p_2 y^2}_{\text{low}} + \underbrace{p_3 y^3 + p_4 y^4}_{y^3 = y+1,\ y^4 = y^2+y} = (p_0+p_3) + (p_1+p_3+p_4)\,y + (p_2+p_4)\,y^2, \]

    where \(p_k = \sum_{i+j=k} a_i b_j\). Those five sums are nine products in \(K\), and Section 5 uses exactly this formula to prove a multiplication. A memory word of leanVM is one element of \(E\), 192 bits, while addresses and counters stay in \(K\).

    Compute \(g^{65}\) and check it against Figure 4.

    \(g^{65} = g \cdot g^{64} = x\,(x^4+x^3+x+1) = x^5+x^4+x^2+x\). In bits that is positions 5, 4, 2, 1: \(\mathtt{0b110110} = \mathtt{36}\). Scrub Figure 4 one step past \(g^{64}\) to see the pattern shift left.

    3The machine

    A virtual machine is a loop: fetch the instruction the program counter points at, execute it, move the counter. leanVM's loop is that and little more, but one design decision changes what "execute" means. The memory is never modified. It is a fixed array chosen by the prover before the run, and an instruction only asserts a relation between some of its words. The run is valid when every assertion holds.

    Definition 2 (state, memory, run)

    The machine has two registers in \(K\), the program counter \(\mathbf{pc}\) and the frame pointer \(\mathbf{fp}\), both starting at \(g^0 = 1\). It reads a public bytecode of \(2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{bc}}}\) instructions, at addresses \(g^0, g^1, \dots\), and a memory \(\mathbf{m}\) of \(2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}}\) words of \(E\), at addresses \(g^0, g^1, \dots\), with \(16 \le \kappa_{\mathrm{mem}} \le 32\). A 256-bit public input fixes the first two words. A run repeats: fetch the instruction at \(\mathbf{pc}\), check its assertion, update \((\mathbf{pc}, \mathbf{fp})\); it succeeds when \((\mathbf{pc}, \mathbf{fp})\) reaches \((g^{2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{bc}}}-1}, g^0)\), the last bytecode address with the initial frame.

    An instruction names memory words by offsets \(o = g^j\) relative to the frame pointer: the operand \(o\) means the word \(\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\cdot o]\), at offset \(j\) in the current frame. There are six instructions.

    InstructionOperandsWhat it asserts
    XOR\(o_A, o_B, o_C\)\(\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_C] = \mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_A] + \mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_B]\), addition in \(E\)
    MUL_NATIVE (MUL)\(o_A, o_B, o_C\)\(\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_C] = \mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_A]\cdot\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_B]\), multiplication in \(E\)
    SET_CONSTANT\(o, k_0, k_1, k_2\)\(\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o] = k_0 + k_1 y + k_2 y^2\)
    DEREF\(o_1, o_2, o_3\); mode\(\mathbf{m}[\,\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_1]\cdot o_2\,] = \) the local word \(\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_3]\), or \(g^2\mathbf{pc}\), or \(\mathbf{fp}\): a store through a pointer
    JUMP\(o_c, o_d, o_f\)if \(\mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_c] \ne 0\): \(\mathbf{pc} \gets \mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_d]\), \(\mathbf{fp} \gets \mathbf{m}[\mathbf{fp}\,o_f]\); all three words must lie in \(K\)
    KECCAK8 offsets, 2 layout bits13 output words are the Keccak-\(f[1600]\) permutation of 13 input words (Section 11)

    Every instruction except a taken JUMP sets \(\mathbf{pc} \gets g\cdot\mathbf{pc}\): "next instruction" is again one multiplication. There is no "load" and no "store" that overwrites: a word that two instructions mention must satisfy both of their assertions.

    The running example

    The rest of the page follows one run of the program in Figure 5, eight bytecode slots (\(\kappa_{\mathrm{bc}} = 3\)) of which six execute, with the public input \(\mathbf{m}[g^0] = \mathtt{5}\) and \(\mathbf{m}[g^1] = 0\). The frame pointer stays \(g^0 = 1\), so the operand \(g^j\) names the word \(\mathbf{m}[g^j]\). The memory is drawn with eight words; a real run has at least \(2^{16}\).

    Three things in Figure 5 recur on every later page.

    Write-once memory is an assertion mechanism. To build the memory, the prover runs the program with every word unset, and an instruction that meets an unset word sets it to the value that makes its assertion true. When an instruction meets a word that is already set, it only checks it. If the program ended with SET m[g³] = 0x7, the run would be invalid: \(\mathbf{m}[g^3]\) already holds \(\mathtt{6}\), and the memory is one fixed array that cannot hold both.

    Hints are free, and untrusted. At step 3 the multiplication \(\mathbf{m}[g^4]\cdot\mathbf{m}[g^2] = \mathbf{m}[g^3]\) has its unknown on the left. The prover computes \(\mathtt{6}\cdot\mathtt{3}^{-1} = \mathtt{2}\) outside the machine and the machine only checks the product: one instruction for a division. In general the memory is a witness: for one bytecode and public input there are many valid memories, and a program can receive any advice through words it never sets itself. The flip side is that a malicious prover chooses those words too, so a program must pin every value it relies on with an assertion. That is the soundness rule for every guest in this repository.

    The end is a place, not an instruction. The run stops when \((\mathbf{pc},\mathbf{fp}) = (g^{7}, g^0)\). The JUMP at step 6 reads a nonzero condition and transfers there. Had the condition been \(0\), the machine would fall through to slot 6, which this program leaves unused.

    Change the public input to \(\mathbf{m}[g^0] = \mathtt{3}\). What happens at steps 2, 3 and 6?

    Step 2 sets \(\mathbf{m}[g^3] = \mathtt{3} + \mathtt{3} = 0\). Step 3 back-solves \(\mathbf{m}[g^4]\cdot\mathtt{3} = 0\), so \(\mathbf{m}[g^4] = 0\). At step 6 the condition is \(0\), so the jump falls through: \(\mathbf{pc} \gets g^6\). Whatever instruction sits in slot 6 then executes, and unless it is a taken jump the counter becomes \(g^7\) with \(\mathbf{fp} = g^0\): the final state, one row later.

    4Four facts about polynomials

    A table of values is a function on bit strings: row \(x \in \{0,1\}^\kappa\) holds the value \(f(x)\). The proof system never checks a table entry by entry. It turns tables into polynomials, and checks polynomials at a single random point. Four facts make that work, and every later section uses them.

    Fact 1: a table is a multilinear polynomial

    Definition 3 (multilinear extension, eq)

    A polynomial is multilinear if each variable appears with degree at most one. Every \(f : \{0,1\}^\kappa \to E\) has exactly one multilinear \(\tilde f\) that agrees with it on \(\{0,1\}^\kappa\), its multilinear extension:

    \[ \tilde f(\class{c-yellow}{r}) = \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^\kappa} f(x)\,\operatorname{eq}(\class{c-yellow}{r}, x), \qquad \operatorname{eq}(r, x) = \prod_{j=1}^{\kappa} \bigl(1 + r_j + x_j\bigr). \]

    Read \(\operatorname{eq}\) one factor at a time. If \(x_j = 1\) the factor is \(r_j\); if \(x_j = 0\) it is \(1 + r_j\), which is \(1 - r_j\) because \(-1 = 1\) in characteristic 2. At a corner \(r \in \{0,1\}^\kappa\) every factor is 1 when \(x = r\) and some factor is 0 otherwise, so \(\operatorname{eq}(r, x) = [x = r]\) and the sum returns \(f(r)\). Between the corners, \(\operatorname{eq}(r, \cdot)\) is a set of weights summing to one, and \(\tilde f(r)\) is the weighted mix of the table. Figure 6 draws this over the real numbers, where the weights are areas you can see.

    The formula also binds variables one at a time. Fixing one variable to \(r\) gives a new table on \(\{0,1\}^{\kappa - 1}\) whose entries are \(f(0, x') + r\,(f(0, x') + f(1, x'))\): the two halves of the table along that variable, mixed by \(r\). (leanVM reads a row index as bits, low bit first. Its sumchecks bind the highest variable first, so the two halves are literally the top and bottom halves of the table; Figure 8 does the same.) That operation is called folding, and it is the inner loop of the sumcheck below, of GKR (Section 7) and of WHIR (Section 10).

    Fact 2: two different polynomials rarely agree at a random point

    Lemma 1 (Schwartz-Zippel)

    A nonzero polynomial over \(E\) of total degree at most \(d\) vanishes at a uniformly random point of \(E^\kappa\) with probability at most \(d / |E|\). So if \(A \ne B\) both have degree at most \(d\), then \(\Pr_r[A(r) = B(r)] \le d / |E|\).

    For one variable this is the statement that a nonzero polynomial of degree \(d\) has at most \(d\) roots; the general case follows by induction on the number of variables. Figure 7 shows why it is the engine of the whole system: to test whether two large objects are equal, encode each as a polynomial and compare one value.

    Fact 3: sumcheck shrinks a sum to one evaluation

    Many checks below have the form "this sum over all \(2^\kappa\) rows equals \(c\)". Recomputing the sum costs the verifier \(2^\kappa\) operations. The sumcheck protocol of Lund, Fortnow, Karloff and Nisan costs it \(\kappa\) rounds of constant work and one evaluation of the summand at a random point.

    Definition 4 (sumcheck)

    To prove \(\sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^\kappa} P(x) = \class{c-purple}{c_0}\): in round \(j\) the prover sends the univariate polynomial \(h_j(Y) = \sum_{x} P(\chi_1, \dots, \chi_{j-1}, Y, x)\), summing over the variables not yet bound. The verifier checks \(h_j(0) + h_j(1) = \class{c-purple}{c_{j-1}}\), draws a challenge \(\class{c-yellow}{\chi_j}\) and sets \(\class{c-purple}{c_j} = h_j(\chi_j)\). After \(\kappa\) rounds it holds the claim \(P(\chi_1, \dots, \chi_\kappa) = c_\kappa\), which something else must settle.

    Figure 8 runs it on an eight-entry table in \(K\), with \(P = \tilde f\). The values and challenges are small elements of \(K\) so that every product stays below bit 64 and the hex is short; the arithmetic is the real field arithmetic of Figure 3.

    Why can't a prover lie? Suppose \(c_0\) is false. To pass round 1 the prover must send some \(h_1 \ne\) the honest \(H_1\) (the honest one sums to the true value). Two different polynomials of degree \(d\) agree at the random \(\chi_1\) with probability at most \(d/|E|\) (Lemma 1); otherwise the new claim \(c_1 = h_1(\chi_1)\) is false again, and the argument repeats. A false claim survives all \(\kappa\) rounds with probability at most \(d\kappa/|E|\).

    Two savings apply to every round message in leanVM. A degree-\(d\) polynomial takes \(d+1\) coefficients, but the check \(h_j(0)+h_j(1) = c_{j-1}\) already fixes one, so \(d\) are sent. And when the summand carries a factor the verifier can compute itself, such as \(\operatorname{eq}(r, \cdot)\), only the other factor's round polynomial is sent, one degree lower (Gruen's trick).

    Fact 4: zerocheck, and claims as inner products

    A table of constraints must be zero on every row. Sum it against \(\operatorname{eq}\) at a random point:

    \[ \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^\kappa} \operatorname{eq}(\class{c-yellow}{r}, x)\, C(x) \;\overset{?}{=}\; 0 . \]

    The left side is \(\tilde C(r)\). If \(C\) is zero on every row, \(\tilde C\) is the zero polynomial and the sum is \(0\) for every \(r\). If some row is nonzero, \(\tilde C\) is a nonzero multilinear and vanishes at the random \(r\) with probability at most \(\kappa/|E|\). Sumcheck then proves the sum. This is a zerocheck, and Section 8 runs one for every constraint of every table at once.

    Every sub-protocol ends on a claim of one shape: a known weight vector \(W\), a committed vector \(g\), and a value.

    Definition 5 (weighted claim)

    To commit to a vector is to publish a short digest (a Merkle root, Section 9) that fixes the vector: the prover cannot change it afterwards. A weighted claim about a committed multilinear \(\class{c-blue}{q} : \{0,1\}^M \to K\) asserts \( \langle W, \class{c-blue}{q}\rangle = \sum_{w \in \{0,1\}^M} W(w)\, \class{c-blue}{q(w)} = \class{c-purple}{c}\), where the verifier can evaluate \(\tilde W\) anywhere in about \(M\) operations. An evaluation claim \(\tilde q(r) = c\) is the special case \(W = \operatorname{eq}(r, \cdot)\).

    An inner-product commitment scheme commits to \(q\) and later proves any such claim. leanVM uses one commitment, to one vector, and proves one weighted claim about it at the very end (Sections 9 and 10). Everything between here and there is a machine for producing weighted claims.

    5From a run to tables

    A run is a sequence of instructions. A polynomial proof wants rectangles of numbers. leanVM follows the M3 model (multi-multiset matching) of the Binius project: each instruction kind gets its own table, each executed instruction is one row of its table, and the rows carry no order. What ties them into one run is not their position but the bus of Section 6.

    Definition 6 (tables, constraints, padding)

    A column of height \(2^\tau\) is a function \(\{0,1\}^\tau \to K\); a table is a set of columns of one height. Table \(T_j\) has a fixed list of columns, of constraints (polynomials of degree at most 2 in the columns that must vanish on every row) and of bus flushes (Section 6). The prover announces each table's number of executed rows \(N_j\); the height is the next power of two \(2^{\tau_j} \ge N_j\), and rows \(N_j, \dots, 2^{\tau_j}-1\) are padding rows, which an honest prover fills with a fixed public row \(\mathbf{p}_j\) (all zeros, except for KECCAK).

    leanVM has six tables, one per instruction. Figure 9 sorts the running example's six rows into them.

    Most tables have no constraints at all. XOR and MUL state their relation inside the tuples they put on the bus: the XOR row reads \(v_A\) and \(v_B\) and announces that the word at \(o_C\) holds \(v_A + v_B\); the MUL row announces \(p_0 + p_3,\ p_1 + p_3 + p_4,\ p_2 + p_4\), the three limbs of the product in \(E\) from Section 2, each of degree 2 in its columns. If the claimed word is not really in memory, the bus does not balance.

    JUMP is the one instruction that needs constraints, because it branches. It carries a boolean column \(b = [v_{\mathrm{cond}} \ne 0]\), and the next program counter it announces is

    \[ \mathbf{pc}' \;=\; \underbrace{b\,v_{\mathbf{pc}}}_{\text{taken}} \;+\; \underbrace{(1 + b)\,g\cdot\mathbf{pc}}_{\text{fall through}} , \]

    written in the specification as \(b\,v_{\mathbf{pc}} + b\,(g\cdot\mathbf{pc}) + g\cdot\mathbf{pc}\). Nothing so far forces \(b\) to be the truth. Two degree-2 constraints do, with the help of a column \(w\) the prover fills with \(v_{\mathrm{cond}}^{-1}\) (or \(0\)):

    \[ C_1:\ b = v_{\mathrm{cond}}\cdot w, \qquad C_2:\ v_{\mathrm{cond}}\,(b + 1) = 0 . \]

    Figure 10 tries every way to set \(b\). In the running example, \(v_{\mathrm{cond}} = \mathtt{2}\) and the honest inverse is \(w = \mathtt{2}^{-1} = \mathtt{800000000000000d}\).

    JUMP also requires the three words it reads to lie in \(K\): its bus tuples carry them as single limbs followed by two zeros. That side effect is used on its own: a JUMP with a zero condition is a free "this word is in \(K\)" check (Section 15).

    What of the padding rows? They are committed like any other row, and a cheating prover could fill them with anything. Section 7 shows that the protocol computes the effect of padding rows from the public row \(\mathbf{p}_j\) alone, so whatever they hold, they cannot put a tuple on the bus.

    6The bus

    Rows that carry no order must still form one run, and must agree on what memory holds. Both are expressed by a single device.

    Definition 7 (bus, balance)

    The bus carries tuples of up to 16 elements of \(K\). Each table has a fixed list of flushes; a flush is a tuple whose entries are polynomials of degree at most 2 in the row's columns, either pushed or pulled. A few boundary tuples are pushed or pulled by the verifier itself. The bus balances when the multiset of pushed tuples equals the multiset of pulled tuples. The first entry of a tuple is a domain separator naming its channel: \(\mathsf{ST} = g^0\) for the machine state, \(\mathsf{MEM} = g^1\) for memory, \(\mathsf{BC} = g^2\) for the bytecode.

    The state channel: rows chain into one run

    Each row pulls the state it starts from, \((\mathsf{ST}, \mathbf{pc}, \mathbf{fp})\), and pushes the state it leaves. The verifier pushes the initial state \((\mathsf{ST}, g^0, g^0)\) and pulls the final one \((\mathsf{ST}, g^{2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{bc}}}-1}, g^0)\). Figure 11 lays the six rows out in a random order and matches tuples.

    Proposition 1 (balance gives a run)

    If the state channel balances, the rows split into a sequence \(r_1, \dots, r_L\) that starts at the initial state, where each row pushes the state the next one pulls, and that ends at the final state, plus possibly some rows forming closed loops.

    Why. Draw one arrow per row, from the state it pulls to the state it pushes, plus one extra arrow from the final state back to the initial one. Balance says every state has as many arrows in as out. A graph with that property is a union of closed walks; the one through the extra arrow, cut at that arrow, is the run. The leftover loops are rows that individually satisfy every assertion and touch memory consistently; they add assertions, never remove one, so they cannot make a false statement true.

    The memory channel: offline memory checking with counters

    Memory is the hard part. A row that reads \(\mathbf{m}[a] = v\) must be telling the truth, and the memory is a committed array of \(2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}}\) words. leanVM uses a lookup argument in the style of Blum et al.'s offline memory checking: every access, including the ones that "write", is a read of the fixed array, and a counter makes reads cancel in a controlled way.

    Definition 8 (lookup flushes)

    • Seed, by the verifier: for every address \(g^i\), push \((\mathsf{MEM}, g^i, 1, \mathbf{m}[g^i])\).
    • Read of \(v\) at \(a\) with count \(c\), by a row: pull \((\mathsf{MEM}, a, c, v)\) and push \((\mathsf{MEM}, a, g\cdot c, v)\).
    • Finalize: for every address, pull \((\mathsf{MEM}, g^i, \mathit{count}^{\mathrm{fin}}[i], \mathbf{m}[g^i])\), with \(\mathit{count}^{\mathrm{fin}}\) a committed column.

    An honest prover gives the \(t\)-th read of an address the count \(g^t\) and sets \(\mathit{count}^{\mathrm{fin}}[i] = g^{A_i}\), where \(A_i\) is the number of reads. Figure 12 follows the address \(g^3\), read twice in the running example.

    Can a cheater make the fake read cancel? Its tuples carry the pair \((g^3, \mathtt{7})\), which no seed or finalize carries, so they can only cancel against other reads of the same pair. Pulled counts \(c\) and pushed counts \(g\cdot c\) must then form the same multiset \(\mathcal N\), that is, \(g\cdot\mathcal N = \mathcal N\). Figure 13 shows what that forces.

    Theorem 1 (lookup correctness)

    If the bus balances, every read count is nonzero, and there are fewer than \(2^{64}-1\) reads, then every read \((a, v)\) is an entry of the array: \(a = g^i\) with \(i < 2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}}\) and \(v = \mathbf{m}[g^i]\).

    The two side conditions are checked directly. Read bound: the verifier caps every table at \(2^{32}\) rows, and a row reads at most 27 times (a KECCAK row: 26 words and its instruction), so at most \(27\cdot 6\cdot 2^{32} < 2^{40}\) reads exist. Nonzero counts: the prover proves the product of all count columns, a single element, and the verifier checks it is not zero. A product is nonzero exactly when every factor is.

    Three consequences matter later. Every address read is in range, so a read through a pointer proves the pointer is some \(g^e\) with \(e < 2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}}\): this is the range check of Section 15. The finalize counts are never checked: a wrong one only unbalances the bus. And the bytecode is a second lookup array on the same bus, with separator \(\mathsf{BC}\) and 11 components (opcode plus ten operands). The program is public, so the verifier computes its entries itself; only its finalize counts, which depend on the run, are committed.

    Address \(g^2\) is read three times in the running example. Which counts appear, and what is \(\mathit{count}^{\mathrm{fin}}\) there?

    The SET, XOR and MUL rows read it with counts \(g^0, g^1, g^2\), pushing \(g^1, g^2, g^3\). The seed pushes \(g^0 = 1\) and the finalize pulls \(\mathit{count}^{\mathrm{fin}} = g^3\). Pushes \(\{g^0, g^1, g^2, g^3\}\), pulls \(\{g^0, g^1, g^2, g^3\}\): balanced.

    7Proving the bus balances

    The bus balances when two multisets of tuples are equal. A proof cannot list the tuples, so it compresses each tuple to one field element and each multiset to one product, and compares products. Both compressions are polynomial identities checked at random points (Lemma 1).

    Definition 9 (fingerprint and product check)

    After the tables are committed, the verifier draws \(\class{c-yellow}{\alpha} \in E^4\) and \(\class{c-yellow}{\beta} \in E\). A tuple \(\mathbf f = (f_0, \dots, f_{15})\), indexed by 4-bit strings \(\iota\), has fingerprint \(\pi_\alpha(\mathbf f) = \sum_{\iota \in \{0,1\}^4} \operatorname{eq}(\class{c-yellow}{\alpha}, \iota)\, f_\iota\), the multilinear extension of the tuple at \(\alpha\). The verifier accepts the bus if

    \[ \prod_{\mathbf f\ \text{pushed}} \bigl(\class{c-yellow}{\beta} - \pi_{\alpha}(\mathbf f)\bigr) \;=\; \prod_{\mathbf f\ \text{pulled}} \bigl(\class{c-yellow}{\beta} - \pi_{\alpha}(\mathbf f)\bigr). \]

    Equal multisets give equal products for every \(\alpha, \beta\). Conversely, each side is a polynomial in the five unknowns \((\alpha, \beta)\) that factors into the degree-one pieces \(\beta - \pi_\alpha(\mathbf f)\); distinct tuples give distinct pieces, so unique factorization recovers the multiset from the polynomial. Different multisets therefore give different polynomials of degree at most \(4\cdot 2^\mu\) (with \(2^\mu\) factors), which agree at the random point with probability at most \(4\cdot 2^\mu/|E|\). Figure 14 computes both products for the running example, in \(E\), and lets you cheat.

    The product itself: GKR on a binary tree

    A product of \(2^\mu\) values is the root of a binary tree whose leaves are the values and whose every node is the product of its two children. The prover announces the root. How can the verifier trust it without the leaves? The GKR protocol of Goldwasser, Kalai and Rothblum walks the tree from the root down, turning a claim about one layer into a claim about the layer below. Write \(V_i\) for layer \(i\) (\(V_0\) the leaves). Since \(V_i(x) = V_{i-1}(0, x)\, V_{i-1}(1, x)\), a claim on \(\tilde V_i\) at a point \(r\) is a sum (Fact 1),

    \[ \class{c-purple}{\tilde V_i(r)} = \sum_{x} \operatorname{eq}(r, x)\; \tilde V_{i-1}(0, x)\; \tilde V_{i-1}(1, x), \]

    which sumcheck (Fact 3) reduces to two values \(\tilde V_{i-1}(0, \chi)\) and \(\tilde V_{i-1}(1, \chi)\) at a random \(\chi\). The prover sends both, a fresh challenge \(u\) blends them into one claim \(\tilde V_{i-1}(u, \chi)\), and the walk continues. Figure 15 runs it on an eight-leaf tree.

    leanVM runs this with two refinements. It contracts two layers at a time (a node is the product of four grandchildren), halving the number of sumchecks, with one binary layer first when \(\mu\) is odd. It proves three trees in one pass: the push side, the pull side and the count tree of Section 6, padded to the same depth, each layer's three sumchecks combined by a random \(\lambda\) into one. The verifier checks that the push and pull roots are one and the same value \(R\) (the balance check) and that the count root \(R_c\) is nonzero. All three trees then leave the pass with claims at one shared point \(\zeta\).

    Who owes what at the leaves

    The leaves of a side are a long vector cut into blocks: one per flush of each table (one leaf per row), one per boundary set, then \(1\)s up to \(2^\mu\). Placed largest first, every block starts at a multiple of its size, so a block sits at a fixed selector, the high bits of its offset. By Fact 1 the leaf claim splits along the blocks:

    \[ \tilde V_0(\zeta) = \sum_{\text{blocks } b} \operatorname{eq}(\mathsf{sel}_b, \zeta_{\ge \kappa_b})\; \tilde L_b(\zeta_{<\kappa_b}) \;+\; \Bigl(1 - \sum_b \operatorname{eq}(\mathsf{sel}_b, \zeta_{\ge\kappa_b})\Bigr). \]

    Figure 16 shows the push side of the running example and colours each block by who settles it.

    One subtlety: padding rows. A table block covers every row of its table, padding included, and the verifier checks it against the leaves

    \[ L_b(x) = \phi_b(x) + [x \ge N_j]\,\bigl(1 - \phi_b(\mathbf p_j)\bigr), \qquad \phi_b(x) = \beta - \pi_\alpha(\mathbf f_{j,f}(x)), \]

    defined from the committed columns whatever they hold. On an executed row, \(L_b = \phi_b\), the honest leaf. On a padding row filled with the public row \(\mathbf p_j\), \(L_b = 1\), a leaf that changes no product. The indicator multiplies a constant, so its share is a public term the verifier computes in \(\tau_j\) steps, and the table sumcheck sees \(\phi_b\) summed over all rows "as if there were no padding". A padding row holding something else gives the leaf \(1 - \pi_\alpha(\mathbf d)\), with \(\mathbf d\) the difference between the tuple it flushes and the tuple \(\mathbf p_j\) flushes: a nonzero polynomial in \(\alpha\) alone, free of \(\beta\). Comparing the top powers of \(\beta\) on both sides shows that such leaves cannot make unequal multisets of real tuples balance (the proposition "padding rows flush nothing" in Section 8 of the specification).

    8One sumcheck for every table

    After GKR the verifier holds, for each of the three sides (push, pull, count), a remainder \(\mathsf{rem}_s\) that the tables owe: a sum over each table's rows of a degree-2 polynomial \(B_j^s\) in its columns, weighted by \(\operatorname{eq}(\zeta_{<\tau_j}, \cdot)\). It also has every constraint \(C_{j,i}\) to zerocheck. A zerocheck needs a random point drawn after the columns were committed, and \(\zeta\) is one: leanVM recycles it. Now every pending statement has the same shape:

    \[ \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^{\tau_j}} \operatorname{eq}(\zeta_{<\tau_j}, x)\; C_{j,i}(x) = 0, \qquad \sum_j \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^{\tau_j}} \operatorname{eq}(\zeta_{<\tau_j}, x)\; B^s_j(x) = \mathsf{rem}_s . \]

    A random \(\class{c-yellow}{\xi}\) batches them, each statement getting its own power \(\xi^k\): the constraints first, numbered table by table (\(o_j\) is the number of constraints in the tables before \(T_j\), \(N_{\mathrm{cons}}\) the total), then one power per product side (\(N_{\mathrm{side}} = 3\): push, pull and count). One difficulty remains: the tables have different heights, and a sumcheck runs over one cube. leanVM multiplies a short table's part by the variables it does not use, \(\prod_{k \ge \tau_j} X_k\). Summed over \(\{0,1\}\), \(X_k\) contributes \(0 + 1 = 1\), so the sum is unchanged:

    \[ F(X) = \sum_j \operatorname{eq}(\zeta_{<\tau_j}, X_{<\tau_j}) \Bigl( \sum_i \xi^{o_j + i - 1} C_{j,i} + \sum_s \xi^{N_{\mathrm{cons}} + s - 1} B^s_j \Bigr)(X_{<\tau_j}) \prod_{k = \tau_j}^{\tau_{\max}-1} X_k , \qquad \sum_{x \in \{0,1\}^{\tau_{\max}}} F(x) \overset{?}{=} \sum_s \xi^{N_{\mathrm{cons}} + s - 1}\, \mathsf{rem}_s . \]

    The rounds bind the highest variable first, so a table of height \(2^{\tau_j}\) does no work until round \(\tau_{\max} - \tau_j\): the sumcheck is back-loaded. Figure 17 shows three tables joining.

    Each summand has degree 3 per variable (degree 2 from the constraint, 1 from \(\operatorname{eq}\)), so a round polynomial has four coefficients, and the round check fixes one: three elements of \(E\) per round. The soundness error is \((3\tau_{\max} + N_{\mathrm{side}} + N_{\mathrm{cons}})/|E|\): three per round, plus one per batched statement for the powers of \(\xi\). Write \(\chi_k\) for the challenge that bound \(X_k\). At the end the prover sends each column's value at \(\chi_{<\tau_j} = (\chi_0, \dots, \chi_{\tau_j - 1})\); the verifier rebuilds every \(C_{j,i}\) and \(B_j^s\) from those values and compares with the last round. The column values become claims in the pool, to be settled by the commitment. Two exceptions: the KECCAK output lanes are never committed, so their claims go to the Keccak argument (Section 11), and the program's own columns are public, so the verifier evaluates them itself.

    In Figure 17, the one-row table never folds. What does its part of \(F\) contribute after the three rounds?

    Its term is \(\operatorname{eq}(\zeta_{<0}, \cdot)\,(\dots)\cdot X_0 X_1 X_2\) with an empty \(\operatorname{eq}\) (equal to 1). After binding \(X_2, X_1, X_0\) to \(\chi_1, \chi_2, \chi_3\) it contributes its single row's batched polynomial times \(\chi_1\chi_2\chi_3\), a factor the verifier computes.

    9Committing: one vector, one code, one root

    Every claim so far is about some column: a table column, a memory limb, a count column. leanVM commits to all of them with one Merkle root, by first gluing them into one vector.

    Stacking

    Sort the committed multilinears \(P_1, P_2, \dots\) by size, largest first, and concatenate their values, padding with zeros to a power of two \(2^M\): \(q = P_1 \Vert P_2 \Vert \dots \Vert 0\). Placed largest first, \(P_i\) of \(2^{\kappa_i}\) values starts at a multiple of \(2^{\kappa_i}\), so its position is a fixed bit string \(\mathsf{sel}_i\), the high \(M - \kappa_i\) bits of its offset, and \(\tilde P_i(z) = \tilde q(z, \mathsf{sel}_i)\). A claim \(\tilde P_i(\zeta) = c\) is therefore the weighted claim \(\langle \operatorname{eq}((\zeta, \mathsf{sel}_i), \cdot),\ q\rangle = c\) on the stack (Definition 5). Figure 18 stacks six columns of the running example (memory, counts and three tables).

    Two remarks. All claims about the stack are combined by a random \(\lambda\) into one, \(\langle \sum_j \lambda^{j-1} W_j, q\rangle = \sum_j \lambda^{j-1} c_j\), so a single opening settles them (Section 14). And the zero padding is not free in general: a table of 5 rows occupies 8. The prover's cost is kept proportional to the placed values, as the next paragraphs show, but padding inside a table remains (a "jagged" commitment would remove it).

    Reed-Solomon encoding

    A Merkle root of \(q\) alone would bind the prover to \(q\), but checking a claim like \(\tilde q(r) = c\) would then need all of \(q\). The fix is redundancy. Split \(q\) into \(2^{\ell_0}\) rows, read each row as the coefficients of a polynomial of degree below \(k\), and evaluate it on a domain of \(n = k/\rho\) points, with rate \(\rho = 2^{-\nu}\) (leanVM uses \(\nu = 1\) by default, so \(n = 2k\)). Two distinct polynomials of degree below \(k\) agree on fewer than \(k\) points, so two different encoded rows differ in more than \(n - k\) positions. A prover that changes one value of \(q\) changes most of the codeword, and a few random spot checks see it.

    The domain must suit a binary field, where "evaluate at the roots of unity" makes no sense. leanVM uses an additive domain, the \(\mathbb F_2\)-span \(\mathcal U_i = \langle 1, x, \dots, x^{i-1}\rangle\): read as 64-bit words, \(\mathcal U_3\) is exactly \(\{\mathtt 0, \dots, \mathtt 7\}\). Its subspace polynomial \(\mathcal V_i(X) = \prod_{u \in \mathcal U_i}(X + u)\) is linear over \(\mathbb F_2\), and its normalization \(\hat{\mathcal V}_i = \mathcal V_i/\mathcal V_i(x^i)\) defines the novel basis of Lin, Chung and Han:

    \[ \mathcal X_j(X) = \prod_{i} \hat{\mathcal V}_i(X)^{\,j_i} \quad (j_i \text{ the bits of } j), \qquad \mathrm{Enc}(a) = \Bigl(\sum_j a_j\, \mathcal X_j(x)\Bigr)_{x \in \mathcal U_{\kappa + \nu}} . \]

    The message is read directly as novel-basis coefficients. In this basis the evaluation splits into even and odd halves exactly like a Cooley-Tukey FFT, with the twiddles \(\hat{\mathcal V}_d(x^c)\) in place of roots of unity: the additive NTT, \(\tfrac12 \kappa n\) multiplications and about \(\kappa n\) additions. Figure 19 encodes four coefficients onto eight points.

    On this small domain the twiddles are small too: \(\hat{\mathcal V}_1\) takes the values \(0,0,1,1,6,6,7,7\) on \(\mathtt0..\mathtt7\), and \(\hat{\mathcal V}_2\) is \(0\) on \(\mathcal U_2\) and \(1\) on the rest. The first four outputs are not the message: the encoding is not systematic, and any four evaluations determine the coefficients by interpolation.

    The committed matrix and its Merkle tree

    The prover encodes each of the \(2^{\ell_0}\) rows (an interleaved codeword) and builds a Merkle tree, with Keccak-256, whose leaves are the columns: leaf \(x\) holds every row's value at the domain point \(x\). One opened leaf then gives the verifier a whole column, which it can fold itself. leanVM takes \(\ell_0 = 6\): 64 rows. Only the rows that hold data are encoded, since encoding zeros gives zeros, and each leaf is hashed starting from its zero padding, whose sponge state is computed once. Both the NTT and the hashing thus cost in proportion to the placed values.

    10Opening: WHIR and Ligerito

    At the end of the protocol the verifier holds a Merkle root and one weighted claim \(\langle W, q\rangle = c\). It must become convinced the claim is true of the committed data. The scheme leanVM uses is WHIR (Arnon, Chiesa, Fenzi, Yogev) instantiated with interleaved codes, which coincides with Ligerito (Novakovic, Angeris) over Reed-Solomon codes.

    What "the committed data" means: lists

    A Merkle root does not prove the matrix under it is a codeword. A cheater could commit a matrix that is a codeword except on a few columns. So the scheme speaks of the codewords close to the committed matrix: within relative distance \(\gamma\) (they agree on at least \((1-\gamma)n\) columns). If \(\gamma\) is under half the minimum distance there is at most one; leanVM works beyond that, in the list-decoding regime, where the Johnson bound caps the list.

    Theorem 2 (Johnson bound)

    For a code of rate \(\rho\) and any slack \(\eta \in (0, 1 - \sqrt\rho)\), every word has at most \(L = \frac{1}{2\eta\sqrt\rho}\) codewords within relative distance \(\gamma = 1 - \sqrt\rho - \eta\).

    The commitment is therefore \(L\)-list binding: it binds the prover to a short list of polynomials, and the opening must fail if no polynomial on the list satisfies the claim. leanVM takes no out-of-domain sample at commitment time, following Flock: for the first oracle the list is enough, because every later check is a union bound over at most \(L\) candidates. Each later level does take one (Figure 21).

    One level of the opening

    Each level shrinks the polynomial by \(\ell\) variables. Figure 21 runs one level.

    Two facts make Figure 21 work. A weighted claim \(\langle W, f\rangle\) is a sum over the cube of \(\tilde W\cdot\tilde f\), so sumcheck (Section 4) applies to it directly. And encoding is linear, so folding the encoded rows with \(\operatorname{eq}(\chi, \cdot)\) gives the encoding of the folded polynomial: column \(x\) of the old matrix, folded, is \(\mathrm{Enc}(f')[x]\). The point of the level is then the last beat. A query's value \(c_q = \mathrm{Enc}(f')[x_q]\) is a linear function of \(f'\)'s coefficients:

    \[ \mathrm{Enc}(f')[x] = \langle W_x, f'\rangle, \qquad \tilde W_x(r) = \prod_{i} \bigl((1 + r_i) + r_i\, \hat{\mathcal V}_i(x)\bigr), \]

    a product of \(\kappa\) factors the verifier computes in \(O(\kappa)\) operations. So the verifier never decodes anything: queries become claims, claims are batched by \(\lambda\), and the batch is the next level's sumcheck. After the last level the remaining polynomial has at most \(2^5\) coefficients; the prover sends it in clear, and the verifier finishes the last sumcheck rounds against it.

    Why it is sound, and how many queries

    The analysis is round by round: an invariant "every codeword near the current oracle violates the current claim" holds on a false statement and survives each verifier message except with small probability. Batching \(J\) claims costs \((J-1)L/|E|\). A fold challenge costs \(2L/|E| + \epsilon_{\mathrm{mca}}\), where \(\epsilon_{\mathrm{mca}}\), the mutual correlated agreement error (Ben-Sasson et al.), bounds the chance that folding creates a new nearby codeword; Jo's interleaving theorem makes it independent of the number of rows. The out-of-domain sample costs \(\binom{L}{2} M / |E|\). The queries cost \((1-\gamma)^{\omega}\): a candidate polynomial whose encoding disagrees with the folded column on at least a \(\gamma\) fraction of points (so it is not on the list) escapes all \(\omega\) queries with that probability. That last term is the only one not divided by \(|E| = 2^{192}\), so it sets the query count. Figure 22 lets you trade rate against queries.

    The production parameters, from pcs/src/whir_config.rs: a 128-bit round-by-round target over \(E\); rate \(1/2\) at level 0 by default (the CLI accepts \(2^{-1}\) to \(2^{-4}\)); a first fold of 6 variables, then folds of 4; the Reed-Solomon domain shrinks by \(2^3\) after the first fold and by \(2\) after each later one, so the rate drops and later levels need fewer queries; folding stops at 5 variables. After Fiat-Shamir, a round-by-round error \(\varepsilon\) costs an attacker \(1/\varepsilon\) hash evaluations per success, which is why each round is analysed separately rather than summed.

    At rate \(1/4\) with \(\eta = 0.05\), how many bits does one query give? Check with Figure 22.

    \(\sqrt\rho = 0.5\), so \(\gamma = 1 - 0.5 - 0.05 = 0.45\) and one query leaves \(1 - \gamma = 0.55\): \(-\log_2 0.55 \approx 0.86\) bits. For 111 bits, \(\lceil 111/0.862\rceil = 129\) queries.

    11Keccak inside the machine

    The guest spends most of its time hashing: every signature check, every Merkle path and every Fiat-Shamir challenge of a child proof is Keccak. Proving each of its bit operations as table rows would be ruinous, so leanVM makes one Keccak-\(f[1600]\) permutation one instruction, and proves all of them together with a dedicated GKR.

    The instruction and its memory layout

    A Keccak state is 25 lanes of 64 bits. A memory word holds three \(K\)-limbs, so KECCAK packs two lanes per word with the top limb zero: 13 words. It takes eight offsets: seven to read from (four single words \(m_0..m_3\), a run of four, a single word, a run of four for the capacity) and one for the 13 consecutive words it writes. Two layouts exist, even and odd, shifted by one lane, because a Keccak-256 block is 136 bytes, 17 lanes, 8.5 words: consecutive blocks of a message alternate between starting on a word boundary and in the middle of one. Figure 23 shows both.

    The KECCAK table has one row per permutation (at least \(2^9\) rows: the Keccak argument works on packs of permutations and needs a minimum batch) and 115 columns: the registers, the eight offsets, the two layout bits, a free limb, 27 read counts (26 words and the instruction), the 25 input lanes, the 25 lanes of the state after 12 rounds (the middle state, explained below) and the 25 output lanes. The output lanes are virtual: the bus flushes read them, but they are never committed. The table has no constraints at all. The claims that the table sumcheck leaves on the output lanes are handed to the Keccak argument, which reduces them to claims on the committed input and middle lanes.

    One round, as polynomials

    Keccak's step names \(\theta, \rho, \pi, \chi, \iota\) are fixed by the standard. Inside this section they mean the Keccak steps; elsewhere \(\rho\) is the code rate, \(\pi\) the fingerprint and \(\chi\) a sumcheck challenge.

    Keccak-\(f\) is 24 rounds. Each applies a linear map \(\mathsf{lin} = \pi\circ\rho\circ\theta\) (column parities, rotations, a lane permutation), then the only nonlinear step \(\chi\), then the round constant \(\iota\). Over \(\mathbb F_2\), with \(b = \mathsf{lin}(a)\) and \(j^{\to k}\) the same bit \(k\) lanes along the row,

    \[ a'[j] = b[j] + \bigl(1 + b[j^{\to 1}]\bigr)\, b[j^{\to 2}] + [\,j < 64\,]\,\mathsf{RC}_R[j] . \]

    So a round is a polynomial of degree 2 in the 1600 input bits. Figure 24 applies \(\chi\) to one row of five lanes, on real data: the low 16 bits of the first row of Keccak-\(f\) applied to the zero state.

    GKR over the rounds

    The argument proves \(2^{\tau}\) permutations at once (\(\tau \ge 9\)). It starts from the table sumcheck's claims on the output lanes at one random point and works on packs: groups of 16 permutations, numbered inside the pack by 4 bits and walks the rounds backwards, like the product GKR of Section 7: each layer turns a claim on a round's output into a claim on its input. A claim has the form "a weighted sum of output bits, with weights on the packs, on the position inside a pack and on the 1600 bit positions, equals \(c\)". The linear map is free: its transpose moves onto the position weights, which stay a product of a lane table and a slice table and are never written out in full. Only \(\chi\) needs a sumcheck.

    The trick that makes \(\chi\) cheap is to do sixteen permutations' ANDs with one multiplication. A reverse multiplication-friendly embedding (Cascudo, Cramer, Xing, Yuan) maps 16 bits to a polynomial of degree at most 23 over \(\mathbb F_2\), such that the product of two embeddings projects back to the coordinatewise AND:

    \[ \mathsf{proj}\bigl(\mathsf{emb}(u)\cdot\mathsf{emb}(v)\bigr) = u \wedge v \qquad (u, v \in \mathbb F_2^{16}). \]

    The construction uses seven coprime irreducible polynomials of degrees 1, 1, 3, 3, 5, 5, 5 (product of degree 23), each carrying one, two or three of the bits through the Chinese remainder theorem, and one more bit on the leading coefficient. Figure 25 is that exact construction, from Annex C of the specification.

    With the embedding, one layer goes like this. The prover sends the 47 coefficients of a polynomial \(G\) that combines, over all groups and positions, the embedded \(\chi\) products; the verifier checks its projection against the claim, then evaluates \(G\) at a random \(\upsilon \in E\). A degree-3 sumcheck over the pack index, the half bit (introduced below) and the 11 position bits reduces that evaluation to three values \(\mathsf v_0, \mathsf v_1, \mathsf v_2\), the input bits read at the three shifts \(j, j^{\to 1}, j^{\to 2}\). A challenge batches the three into one claim of the original shape, one round earlier. After the layers, a short sumcheck over the 16 permutations of a pack and the lane slot leaves 64 values: one evaluation of each bit slice \(\tilde Q_z\) of the committed states, at one point. Ring switching takes those (Section 12).

    Committing the middle state

    Twenty-four layers make a long proof and a long recursive verification. leanVM commits the state after 12 rounds as well, in slots 25 to 49 of the packed commitment \(q_{\mathrm{keccak}}\) (so a slot index has 6 bits). A permutation is now two independent halves of 12 rounds: input to middle, middle to output. Both halves are claimed at the same random point and folded by two challenges into one claim, so 12 layers prove both at once. One extra check closes the gap: the value claimed for the middle state must equal the committed slots 25 to 49, a sumcheck over the slot. Figure 26 compares the two shapes.

    The whole argument, from the output claims to the two final claims on \(q_{\mathrm{keccak}}\), fails to catch a false statement with probability at most \((37\tau + 902)/|E|\): \(\tau + 6\) for the starting point, \(3\tau + 72\) per layer (46 for \(\upsilon\), since \(G\) has degree 46, three per sumcheck round, two for the batching), and 32 for the closing sumchecks. With \(|E| = 2^{192}\) and \(\tau \le 32\), that is below \(2^{-180}\).

    12Ring switching: from bits to K

    Keccak states are committed packed: the 64 bits of a lane are one element of \(K\), \(q_{\mathrm{keccak}}(u) = \sum_{i<64} Q_i(u)\, x^i\), where \(Q_i\) is the bit slice \(i\). The Keccak argument ends with 64 claims \(s_i = \tilde Q_i(r)\), one per slice, at a shared point \(r\). The commitment only proves weighted claims about \(q_{\mathrm{keccak}}\) itself. Ring switching (Diamond and Posen) bridges the gap with one random linear map.

    Take any \(\mathbb F_2\)-linear map \(\Phi : E \to E\). Because the bits \(Q_i(u)\) are 0 or 1, \(\Phi\) passes through the sum over \(u\):

    \[ \sum_u \Phi\bigl(\operatorname{eq}(r, u)\bigr)\, q_{\mathrm{keccak}}(u) = \sum_{i<64} x^i \sum_u Q_i(u)\,\Phi\bigl(\operatorname{eq}(r,u)\bigr) = \sum_{i<64} x^i\, \Phi(\class{c-purple}{s_i}). \]

    The left side is one weighted claim on \(q_{\mathrm{keccak}}\), with weight \(W(u) = \Phi(\operatorname{eq}(r,u))\); the right side is a target the verifier computes from the 64 values. The question is which \(\Phi\) makes this sound. Figure 27 shows that the obvious choice fails.

    Every \(\mathbb F_2\)-linear map of \(E\) is a sum of Frobenius powers, \(\Phi(a) = \sum_{k} c_k\, a^{2^k}\), since squaring is additive in characteristic 2. Write each error \(\delta_i = s_i - \tilde Q_i(r)\) in the 192 bits of \(E\), and collect bit \(w\) of all 64 errors into \(\Delta_w = \sum_i \delta_{i,w} x^i \in K\). Exchanging the sums, the cheater wins when \(\sum_k c_k \Omega_k = 0\), with \(\Omega_k = \sum_w e_w^{2^k}\Delta_w\) (\(e_w\) the bit basis of \(E\)): the \(\Omega_k\) depend only on the errors, the \(c_k\) only on the randomness. A counting argument shows that 64 Frobenius terms are necessary and sufficient for every nonzero error to leave some \(\Omega_k \ne 0\): it reduces to a polynomial of degree 63 vanishing at the 64 distinct conjugates \(x, x^2, x^4, \dots, x^{2^{63}}\) of \(x\).

    Drawing 64 independent coefficients would be expensive to apply. leanVM draws six challenges \(f_0, \dots, f_5\) and composes six two-term maps,

    \[ a_{p+1} = a_p + f_p\, a_p^{\,2^{2^{5-p}}}\quad (0 \le p < 6), \qquad \Phi(a) = a_6, \]

    which expand to 64 terms whose coefficients are 64 distinct monomials in the \(f_p\) (one per binary digit pattern of \(k\)). The cheater's polynomial in \(f_0..f_5\) is nonzero of degree below \(2^{32}\), so Schwartz-Zippel bounds the error by \(2^{32}/2^{192} = 2^{-160}\). One application costs 63 squarings and 6 multiplications in \(E\). And the verifier evaluates the weight's extension at the opening's point \(r'\) in closed form, since Frobenius passes through \(\operatorname{eq}\) on bits:

    \[ \tilde W(r') = \sum_{k<64} c_k \prod_{n} \bigl(1 + r_n^{2^k} + r'_n\bigr). \]

    13Fiat-Shamir: the conversation becomes a string

    Every check so far assumed a verifier who draws its challenges after the prover's messages. A proof is sent once, with no verifier present. Fiat-Shamir replaces each challenge by a hash of everything sent so far. leanVM implements it as a duplex sponge over Keccak-\(f[1600]\), in overwrite mode, after Plonky3's challenger: lanes 0 to 15 are the rate, lanes 16 to 24 the capacity (576 bits), which no message ever touches directly.

    The details each close a specific hole. The statement first: the IV and public input enter before any challenge, so no challenge can be drawn for one statement and reused for another. The IV hashes a fixed-length tag followed by the bytecode, and Keccak's padding binds the length, so no length field is needed. Tags: a scalar is written as its three limbs followed by a nonzero tag (1 for a scalar, 4 for a grinding nonce), so a sent zero never looks like the zeroed rest of the rate. One permutation per challenge: up to four waiting scalars and the next challenge share one permutation. The recursion guest replays a transcript as one KECCAK instruction per permutation, with the waiting scalars as its message words and the previous output's capacity as its capacity words. Grinding: a proof of work of \(b\) bits absorbs a nonce and draws a challenge whose low \(b\) bits must be zero; WHIR grinds 17 bits before each query phase. Merkle trees use Keccak-256 itself, separately from the sponge.

    After Fiat-Shamir, an attacker can retry a round by re-hashing. That is why every error bound on this page is per round: a round with error \(\varepsilon\) costs about \(1/\varepsilon\) Keccak evaluations to break, and the system is designed so that every round has \(\varepsilon \le 2^{-128}\).

    14The whole proof, in order

    One piece is still missing: the public input. It must sit in the first two memory words, \(\mathbf m[g^0] = \mathsf{in}_0 + \mathsf{in}_1 y\) and \(\mathbf m[g^1] = \mathsf{in}_2 + \mathsf{in}_3 y\). Those words sit at the points \((0, 0, \dots, 0)\) and \((1, 0, \dots, 0)\) of the memory's cube, so both conditions are one degree-1 identity in a single variable:

    \[ \tilde{\mathbf m}(X, 0, \dots, 0) = (1 + X)(\mathsf{in}_0 + \mathsf{in}_1 y) + X(\mathsf{in}_2 + \mathsf{in}_3 y). \]

    The verifier draws \(r_m\), computes the right side limb by limb, and adds three claims on the memory limbs \(\mathbf m_0, \mathbf m_1, \mathbf m_2\) at \((r_m, 0, \dots, 0)\) to the pool. The prover sends nothing for this step. A wrong input word makes the two sides distinct lines in \(r_m\), equal at the random point with probability \(1/|E|\).

    Every part is now on the table. Figure 29 replays a complete proof as the conversation it compiles from, with the claim pool filling on the right.

    Read Figure 29 as the answer to the question of Section 1. The verifier's work is: hash the transcript (one Keccak permutation per challenge), check a few dozen sumcheck rounds, evaluate public polynomials (selectors, the index column, the padding terms, the program's bytecode at one point), and check a few hundred Merkle paths. None of it grows with the length of the execution except logarithmically. The prover's work is the opposite: commit the full trace once, then answer each challenge.

    The core idea

    leanVM turns "this program ran correctly on this input" into one weighted-sum claim about one committed vector. Each step on the way tests a polynomial identity at a point chosen after the data it depends on was fixed, and hands a smaller claim of the same shape to the next step.

    The setup has one more job. The prover announces the memory size \(\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}\), each table's row count and the rate exponent, and the verifier checks them against public caps (at most \(2^{32}\) rows per table, \(2^{32}\) memory words, \(2^{32}\) instructions) before deriving the layout of every stack and every leaf vector. The caps are what make the read bound of Theorem 1 hold. And the derivation is identical in the prover and in all three verifiers (Section 17): any difference would make an honest proof fail.

    15Programming six instructions

    The guests are written in a zkDSL, a Python-shaped language (crates/lean_compiler/zkDSL.md) whose compiler targets the six instructions. Two ideas run through every pattern it uses: write-once memory as an assertion mechanism, and indices in the exponent. Here are the patterns, from the specification's Section 9.

    Division, inequality, and hints

    Division \(q = a/b\) is one MUL with \(q\) as the unset factor: the runner fills \(q = a b^{-1}\), the table proves \(q b = a\) (Figure 5, row 3). An inequality \(a \ne b\) costs three instructions and one hint:

    1 XOR t = a + b # t = 0 exactly when a = b 2 MUL p = t · w # w is a hint, honestly t⁻¹ 3 SET p = 1 # second assertion on the same word

    Both line 2 and line 3 assert the value of \(p\). If \(a = b\), then \(t = 0\) and \(p = 0\) whatever the hint, and the SET conflicts. Every security-relevant hint must be pinned like this: the prover chooses all unset words.

    Function calls

    A frame has the layout \([\mathit{retpc}, \mathit{retfp}, \mathrm{args}, \mathrm{rets}, \mathrm{locals}]\), relative to its frame pointer. Figure 30 makes a call with one argument and one return value.

    Because \(\mathit{nfp}\) is a hint, a function's behaviour must not depend on where its frame lands; the convention is that it depends only on its inputs. The ISA would allow a frame pointer outside the memory range as long as every access \(\mathbf{fp}\cdot o\) lands inside, but the calling convention keeps \(\mathbf{fp}\) in range for free: on a call through the return-address store, on a return by induction.

    Loops, branches, and checks in \(K\)

    A counted loop keeps its counter in the exponent, from \(g^{\mathrm{lo}}\), times \(g\) per iteration, until \(g^{\mathrm{hi}}\). It is compiled as a tail-recursive helper whose exit test is the recursive call's own JUMP condition, \(i + g^{\mathrm{hi}}\), nonzero exactly while the loop continues: one call plus two instructions per iteration. An if on an equality is one XOR and one JUMP to the branch the test should not fall into, so no negation is computed. A taken JUMP reloads \(\mathbf{fp}\) from a word, and the ISA has no "read \(\mathbf{fp}\)" instruction, so a branching function first stores its own \(\mathbf{fp}\) with a DEREF in mode fp. Two branches can write the same word, since only one runs. Finally, a JUMP with a zero condition checks that its other two words lie in \(K\), and falls through.

    Range checks in the exponent

    To prove \(x = g^e\) with \(e < k\), in three instructions: a DEREF through \(x\) proves \(x = g^e\) with \(e < 2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}}\), because the bus only balances at real addresses; a MUL \(x\cdot y = g^{k-1}\) back-solves the complement \(y\); a DEREF through \(y\) proves \(y = g^f\) with \(f < 2^{\kappa_{\mathrm{mem}}}\). Then \(e + f \equiv k - 1 \pmod{2^{64}-1}\). Figure 31 shows why that congruence is an equality.

    Soundness holds for any \(k\), since \(e + f < 2^{33}\). The bound \(k \le 2^{16}\), the smallest memory, is for completeness: it guarantees that an honest complement \(f \le k - 1\) is a valid address whatever memory size the prover announces. A match on \(x = g^j\) uses the same addresses: a jump table of \(n\) words in main's frame (always at \(g^0\)), a DEREF reads entry \(j\) at \(x\cdot g^t\) and a JUMP goes there: three cycles in all, for any \(n\). It does not check \(j < n\), so untrusted indices are range-checked first.

    Hashing

    A Keccak-256 block is 8.5 words, so block \(k\) of a message stored in consecutive words starts at word \(\lfloor 17k/2\rfloor\), on the high limb when \(k\) is odd, and matches the state layout of \(k\)'s parity (Figure 23): at most nine XORs into the previous output and one KECCAK per block. A list of \(n\) claims of 64 bytes each is absorbed 17 at a time (17 claims are exactly four block pairs), with \(n = 17b + r\) supplied as a hint and pinned by range checks \(r < 17\), \(b < 2^{16}\) and \(g^{17b + r} = g^n\).

    16What runs on it: signatures, blobs, recursion

    One guest program, crates/rec_aggregation/guests/lean_ethereum.py, does everything leanVM is for. It checks raw signatures, checks blob encodings, and verifies child proofs of itself. Its public input is a 32-byte hash of a fixed-width statement.

    leanXMSS: hash-based signatures for validators

    XMSS (RFC 8391) signs with one-time keys at the leaves of a Merkle tree. leanXMSS stacks two layers of height 16, so one key signs at \(2^{32}\) leaf indices, and uses WOTS+C one-time keys over Keccak-256. It is stateful: an index may sign only one message. For validators that is natural, since signing the same epoch twice is already a slashable offence. Figure 32 follows one signature.

    WOTS+C replaces the Winternitz checksum. In plain Winternitz signatures a checksum stops a forger from walking revealed chains forward. In WOTS+C the signer instead grinds a 16-byte randomizer until the digest is a codeword: its last six base-4 digits are zero and the first 58 sum to exactly 106. Two distinct codewords with the same sum are incomparable, each has a digit smaller than the other's, so no revealed set signs a second message. A codeword appears with probability about \(2^{-20}\), so signing costs about a million Keccak permutations (amortized over the top layer), and verifying costs 186: per layer, 1 for the digest, \(3\cdot 58 - 106 = 68\) for the chains, 8 for the leaf and 16 for the path. Signatures are 2404 bytes, public keys 32.

    leanSPHINCS: stateless signatures for users

    Users cannot keep a counter safely, so transactions need a stateless scheme. leanSPHINCS is SPHINCS+ over Keccak-256: the message digest picks a leaf and a FORS key (a few-time scheme: 24 Merkle trees of height 10, one revealed leaf each, encoding the digest), and a hypertree, three stacked layers of WOTS+C trees, of heights 8, 8 and 10 signs that FORS key up to the public key. A signature is 7740 bytes and verifies in 492 permutations. (The SPHINCS+ lesson in this series covers the scheme in detail.) Both schemes are specified in doc/lean_xmss and doc/lean_sphincs, with Lean 4 proofs of 127-bit classical security in the random-oracle model in formal/.

    LeanDA: proving a blob is correctly encoded

    Data availability sampling lets a node check that blob data can be retrieved by downloading a few random pieces of an erasure-coded version. Sampling says nothing about whether those pieces form a valid encoding; KZG made validity automatic, and a Merkle root does not. LeanDA (Meng, Wagner, Kadianakis, Risitano) proves validity with a STARK instead, and leanVM's version reuses its own Reed-Solomon code over \(K\).

    QuantityValueMeaning
    \(k\)\(2^{14}\) symbols128 KiB of payload per blob (EIP-4844 size)
    \(n\)\(2^{15}\) symbols256 KiB per encoded row, rate 1/2
    cell\(2^8\) symbols2 KiB, the sampling unit (EIP-7594 size); 128 cells per row, any 64 rebuild it

    A blob is the \(k\) evaluations of a polynomial of degree below \(k\) on \(\mathcal U_{14}\), and its encoding is the evaluations on \(\mathcal U_{15}\), so the first half is the blob itself. (This code is systematic because the blob is given by evaluations; the PCS of Section 9 reads its rows as coefficients instead.) Each row gets a Merkle tree over its 128 cell hashes, and the commitment is a Merkle root over the row roots. The guest recomputes that root and checks every row is a codeword. That check is where the additive domain pays off. Over an additive domain of size \(n\), the values of any polynomial of degree at most \(n - 2\) sum to zero (the specification's Annex D). The product of two polynomials of degree below \(k = n/2\) has degree at most \(n - 2\), so any two codewords are orthogonal, and counting dimensions shows the code is exactly its own dual: a word is a codeword precisely when it is orthogonal to every codeword. One random codeword suffices:

    \[ \mathsf L(X) = \prod_{j < 14} \bigl(1 + \class{c-yellow}{z}^{2^j}\, \hat{\mathcal V}_j(X)\bigr), \qquad \langle \mathsf L, w_i\rangle = \sum_{x \in \mathcal U_{15}} \mathsf L(x)\, w_i(x) \overset{?}{=} 0 \ \text{ for every row } w_i . \]

    Expanded, \(\mathsf L = \sum_u z^u \mathcal X_u\) has degree below \(k\), so it is a codeword and is orthogonal to every valid row. For an invalid row, \(\langle \mathsf L, w\rangle\) is a nonzero polynomial in \(z\) of degree below \(k\), so a random \(z \in E\) misses with probability at most \((k-1)/|E| < 2^{-178}\). Figure 34 runs the test on a codeword of length 8.

    The challenge \(z\) is derived from the root itself by the VM's transcript (IV: the hash of leanDA/rs-membership/v1), and the native verifier recomputes it outside the proof, as the security proof requires. The guest takes \(z\) as a hint, builds \(\mathsf L\) once as a product tree over the bits of \(x\), and the statement carries the digest \(H(\mathsf{root}, z)\).

    Recursion: a proof that verifies proofs

    A node publishes signature claims (pairs of public key and message, one sorted list per scheme) and at most one DA root. It may publish any subset of what its raw signatures and verified children support; the rule is coverage: every published claim is justified at this node or by a child. Figure 35 assembles a small aggregation tree.

    The statement is the Keccak-256 hash of fixed-width fields: the Fiat-Shamir IV (32 bytes), the leanXMSS claim-list digest (32), the leanSPHINCS claim-list digest (32), the DA digest \(H(\mathsf{root}, z)\) or zeros (32), and the deferred bytecode point and value (\((\kappa_{\mathrm{bc}}+5)\times 24\) bytes). Fixed widths make the preimage parse one way. Coverage is enforced with a slot table: one region per scheme, the published claims first, then extra slots for duplicates and omitted claims; the prover assigns every supporting occurrence to a slot, and the guest writes a distinct counter into each. Write-once memory forbids two occurrences in one slot, and requiring as many writes as slots means every published claim is covered.

    Two practical details close the loop. The guest verifies proofs of itself, so its bytecode, which the IV hashes, must be known before it is compiled: unified_guest compiles it to a fixed point on its own size. And a child proof must commit at least a minimum number of values for the guest to open it, so the prover grows the memory, never a table, when a proof is too small. aggregate does not verify its children: it only replays them to build the guest's witness, and the guest's in-VM verification rejects a bad one. A caller holding an unchecked child calls EthereumProof::verify on it first.

    Each step of Figure 29 has its own error, and after Fiat-Shamir what matters is the worst round, since an attacker can retry any single round by re-hashing. Figure 36 collects them, as \(-\log_2\) of the probability that a false statement survives that step.

    18What to remember

    1. leanVM is a six-instruction machine over \(K = \mathbb F_{2^{64}}\) and \(E = \mathbb F_{2^{192}}\), with write-once memory: instructions assert, hints are free, and every hint must be pinned.
    2. Indices live in the exponent: addresses, the program counter and counters are powers of \(g = x\), so stepping and pointer arithmetic are multiplications.
    3. A run becomes one table per instruction. Rows carry no order; a bus of pushed and pulled tuples chains them (state), checks memory (lookup with counters in the exponent) and the program (bytecode lookup).
    4. The bus balances when two products of \(\beta - \pi_\alpha(\mathbf f)\) agree; GKR proves the products, padding rows cannot inject tuples, and nonzero counts stop self-cancelling reads.
    5. One back-loaded sumcheck proves every constraint and every table's share of the bus, at the point GKR left.
    6. Keccak is one instruction per permutation, proved by a GKR over the rounds with sixteen permutations per carry-less product, the middle state committed to halve the depth, and ring switching to return from bits to \(K\).
    7. Everything is one stacked vector, Reed-Solomon encoded with the additive NTT and committed by a Keccak Merkle root; every claim lands in one pool, and one WHIR/Ligerito opening settles it.
    8. Fiat-Shamir is a Keccak duplex sponge seeded with the statement; each challenge is one permutation, which makes the recursive verifier cheap.
    9. On top run leanXMSS, leanSPHINCS, LeanDA's membership test and recursive aggregation, all in one guest that verifies proofs of itself.
