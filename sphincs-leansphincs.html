Post-quantum signatures for Ethereum

    SPHINCS+ and leanSphincs

    A signature scheme built from nothing but a hash function. This page first shows the whole scheme in one picture, then opens each of its three layers, then explains why a key wears out and how leanSphincs sets its parameters.

    You need: a hash function \(H\). It maps any input to a short string (16 bytes here), it is fast to compute, and nobody can run it backwards or find two inputs with the same output.
    You will build: a SPHINCS+ signature, layer by layer, the reason a key can only sign so many times, and the choices behind leanSphincs.

    1The whole scheme in one picture

    Ethereum signs with ECDSA and BLS, which a large quantum computer breaks. A hash-based signature relies only on \(H\). SPHINCS+ builds one from three layers, each signing the layer below it. Figure 1 builds them on a toy key and then signs the message “hello”. Every later figure reuses this toy key.

    Algorithm · sign and verify

    Sign a message \(\class{c-yellow}{m}\):

    1. Pick a randomizer \(R\) and hash: \(D = H(R, m)\). The bits of \(D\) give a leaf index and \(k\) FORS indices.
    2. FORS at that leaf reveals the \(k\) chosen secrets with their paths. This signs \(D\).
    3. The leaf's WOTS key signs the FORS public key.
    4. Attach the leaf's Merkle path. The signature is \(R\), the FORS signature, the WOTS signature and the path.

    Verify: recompute \(D\), rebuild the FORS public key from the revealed secrets, rebuild the WOTS public key from its signature, hash it into the leaf, climb the path, and compare with the public key.

    Each layer answers one question. Why hash chains? They make a one-time signature (Section 3). Why a tree? It puts many one-time keys under one public key (Section 4). Why FORS in the middle? The leaf is chosen by the message, so leaves get reused, and only a few-time scheme survives that (Section 5).

    3Layer 1: a one-time signature from hash chains

    The WOTS key in the middle of Figure 1 is made of hash chains. Pick a random secret \(\class{c-red}{x_0}\), hash it again and again, and publish only the top. Revealing a point on the chain signs a digit. Figure 3 does this with real SHA-256 values.

    Definition 1 · Winternitz one-time signature (WOTS)

    Hash the message to \(\ell\) digits \(\class{c-yellow}{d_1,\dots,d_\ell}\) in base \(w\), one chain per digit. The signature reveals the point \(d_i\) steps up chain \(i\). The verifier hashes each point to the top of its chain and compares with the public key.

    To stop digits being raised, accept only digit vectors with a fixed sum: raising one digit then forces lowering another, which means inverting \(H\). WOTS+ NIST FIPS 205 enforces this with extra checksum chains. WOTS+C paper SPHINCS+C drops them: the signer appends a counter \(c\) and tries \(c = 0, 1, 2, \dots\) until the digits hit the target sum (Figure 4).

    leanSphincs proposal sphincs.art uses 64 chains of length 4 with target sum 120, above the average sum of 96. The verifier then walks only \(64 \cdot 3 - 120 = 72\) chain steps, and the signer tries about 830 counters, since a random digest hits 120 with probability \(1/829\). The WOTS+C signature is 64 values of 16 bytes plus a 4-byte counter: 1,028 bytes.

    4Layer 2: one public key for many one-time keys

    A WOTS key signs once. To sign many times, make \(2^{h}\) of them and hash their public keys pairwise into a Merkle tree. The root is the public key. A signature adds the authentication path: the \(h\) siblings the verifier needs to climb from the leaf to the root (Figure 5). The digest of “hello” picked leaf 0 in Figure 1, so Figure 5 signs with leaf 0.

    5Layer 3: why FORS sits between the message and WOTS

    Signing leaf by leaf with a counter gives XMSS, which breaks if a leaf is ever used twice, for instance after restoring an old backup. SPHINCS+ keeps no counter: the leaf is read from the digest of the message. Figure 6 shows the consequence. Random leaves repeat long before they run out; with \(2^{26}\) leaves, after the order of \(\sqrt{2^{26}} = 8{,}192\) signatures.

    That is why WOTS never signs the message. It signs the FORS public key of its own leaf, which never changes, so a reused leaf signs the same value again and leaks nothing new. The message is signed by FORS, which tolerates a few uses.

    Definition 2 · FORS (forest of random subsets)

    Take \(k\) Merkle trees of height \(a\) whose leaves are hashes of secrets. Cut the digest into \(k\) indices of \(a\) bits. In tree \(j\), reveal secret number \(i_j\) and its path. The FORS public key is the hash of the \(k\) roots.

    7Why a key wears out

    Each FORS signature puts \(k\) secrets in public, and they never go away. A forger wins by finding a new message whose FORS indices all point at revealed secrets. Four figures build that risk up: one leaf of the toy key, the odds as a product, the real 24 trees, and the leaves that matter most.

    The forger's game on one leaf

    Figure 9 takes the toy FORS of leaf 0. Three messages land on that leaf and are signed. Then a forger hashes candidate messages until one points only at revealed secrets.

    The odds are a product over trees

    Each tree reads its index from different bits of the digest, so the trees are independent and the odds multiply. After \(r\) signatures, a given secret in a tree of \(2^{a}\) is still hidden with probability \((1-2^{-a})^{r}\):

    \[ \Pr[\text{one forgery try succeeds}] \;=\; \Big(\underbrace{1-\big(1-2^{-\class{c-orange}{a}}\big)^{\class{c-red}{r}}}_{\text{revealed fraction of one tree}}\Big)^{\class{c-orange}{k}} \]

    Example 1 · the toy and the real FORS

    1. Toy, \(a = 3\), \(r = 3\): expected revealed fraction \(1 - (7/8)^3 = 169/512 \approx 0.33\). Figure 9 drew 2/8, 3/8, 3/8; tree 1 got secret 1 twice, which is why the formula is not \(r/2^a\).
    2. Real, \(a = 10\), \(r = 16\): \(1 - (1023/1024)^{16} = 0.0155\) per tree.
    3. All \(k = 24\) trees: \(0.0155^{24} = 2^{-144.3}\).

    Figure 10 draws the real FORS of one leanSphincs leaf proposal sphincs.art: 24 trees, each a 32 × 32 grid of its 1,024 secrets. Raise \(r\) and fire forgery attempts.

    The unlucky leaves set the security

    Signatures land on random leaves, so after \(N\) signatures the average leaf has signed \(\lambda = N/2^{26}\) times, but some have signed far more: a leaf has \(r\) signatures with the Poisson probability \(e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{r}/r!\). A forgery try also lands on a random leaf, so its success chance is Example 1 averaged over leaves. Heavy leaves are rare but much weaker. Figure 11 puts both costs in bits.

    The lifetime

    Repeat Figure 11 for every \(N\). The lifetime is the largest \(N\) that keeps 127 bits, the NIST level 1 target the leanSphincs note uses (Figure 12).

    8Same layers, different shapes: the variants

    Every variant has the three layers of Figure 1. They differ in how the tree is built and how big FORS is. SLH-DSA-128s NIST FIPS 205 targets \(2^{64}\) signatures, too many leaves to build as one tree, so it stacks 7 trees of height 9 into a hypertree: each tree's WOTS keys sign the roots of the trees below. SLH-DSA-128-24 NIST draft SP 800-230 targets \(2^{24}\) and uses one tree, and leanSphincs proposal sphincs.art keeps one tree for \(2^{30}\). Figure 13 draws the three shapes.

    Figure 14 compares sizes. SLH-DSA-128-24 is smallest, but its FORS trees have height 24, so the signer hashes 16.8 million leaves per tree. leanSphincs's trees have 1,024 leaves, and its whole FORS signing costs about 61,000 compressions.

    The WOTS-forest variant proposal leanSphincs note replaces FORS with two levels of small WOTS keys: 25% smaller and 18% cheaper to verify at the same signing cost. The idea came from Jonas Nick's submission to sig.golf leaderboard sig.golf, a leaderboard run for lean Ethereum that scores stateless hash-based schemes by bytes times RISC-V verification cycles and checks each entry's security in Lean. Its baseline is a SPHINCS+ variant at 6,404 bytes × 11,573 cycles; on 22 October 2026 the best entry scored 5,239 bytes × 6,963 cycles.

    9Pruning: build only part of the tree

    A full leanSphincs key has \(2^{26}\) leaves to generate: about \(16 \cdot 10^{9}\) compressions, 16 seconds on a laptop and about 6 days on a hardware wallet. Hypertree pruning blog conduition builds one subtree of \(2^{b}\) leaves and fills the missing siblings above it with pseudorandom surrogates. The root looks like any other public key (Figure 15).

    Every retry costs one compression, \(2^{26-b}\) on average, and every FORS sees \(2^{26-b}\) times more signatures, which shifts Figure 12's curve left. The leanSphincs proposal picks three points on that trade proposal sphincs.art:

    10What to remember

    • Three layers: FORS signs the message, a WOTS one-time key signs the FORS key, a Merkle path links the WOTS key to the public root.
    • WOTS is hash chains with a fixed digit sum. The tree turns many one-time keys into one public key.
    • The message picks the leaf, so leaves repeat; that is why a few-time FORS sits in the middle.
    • FORS leaks \(k\) secrets per signature. A forgery needs leaked secrets in all \(k\) trees, and rare heavily used leaves set the security: about \(2^{30}\) signatures at 127 bits for leanSphincs.
    • FIPS 205 is the NIST standard (hypertree), SP 800-230 a NIST draft (one tree, \(2^{24}\)), leanSphincs a proposal on sphincs.art, sig.golf a leaderboard, pruning a blog technique.
